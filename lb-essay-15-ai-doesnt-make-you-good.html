AI Doesn't Make You Good. It Makes You Faster.

The draft used to take an afternoon. Now it takes ten minutes, and it looks finished either way — which is exactly the problem.

The draft used to take an afternoon. Now it takes ten minutes, and it looks finished before I've read a single line of it.

That part isn't hype. I've watched it happen enough times to trust it completely. What I've stopped trusting is the finished part.

What the Tool Actually Copies

AI doesn't know the difference between my good instinct and my bad guess. It takes whichever one I hand it and gives it back faster, cleaner, and more sure of itself than either one deserves. If what I bring is real — years of knowing how this particular kind of work is supposed to go — I get back good work at speed. If what I bring is a guess dressed up as a plan, I get back a faster, more confident-looking guess. The tool did its job either time. It just doesn't tell me which time it was.

I've watched two people run the same prompt against the same problem — one who's done the work fifteen years, one who started in the spring. Both get something usable back inside a minute. One reads it and knows in three seconds which line is wrong. The other ships it as written, because it reads finished, and finished used to be a decent proxy for correct.

Same tool. Opposite outcome. The software was never the variable.

The Part That Used to Show

Before, when I got something wrong doing it by hand, the wrongness usually looked wrong. A sloppy report looked sloppy. A thin argument felt thin on the page before anyone read it aloud. The rough edges were a signal — they told me exactly where to go back and check.

AI sands the rough edges off before I ever see them. The wrong version comes back in the same font as the right one, the same confident paragraph breaks, the same clean little table. Nothing about how it looks tells me something underneath it is off.

The itch that catches it is the same one I learned reading a room before anyone at the bar said a word, and later reading a production line the same way — something's wrong, arriving before I can say what it is. That itch didn't come from a course. It came from getting it wrong enough times, in enough different rooms, that wrong started to have a shape I could feel before I could explain it.

AI doesn't have that itch, and it can't hand it to me. What it can do is move faster than my itch can keep up with — fine on the days the itch is there to catch it, a quiet problem on the days it isn't and I don't notice until later.

The question that actually matters

Not which tool to use. Whether I know this particular work well enough to catch it when it's confidently wrong about it — because it will be, sooner or later, and it won't sound any different when it is.

If the answer is yes, this has been one of the better things to happen to how I work in years. If I'm not sure — that's worth knowing before I build anything that matters on top of it.

The speed was always going to be the easy part. The judgment was mine before any of this existed. AI didn't hand it to me. It just made the absence of it move faster too.
