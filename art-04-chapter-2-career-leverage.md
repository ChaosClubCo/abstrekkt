## The four projects in this phase

### Project 2A — Positioning Statement (Week 3)

**The problem:** Anyone asking "what do you do?" gets a different answer depending on who's asking. That's not flexibility, that's drift. Drift kills referrals and kills external pitches.

**The deliverable:** One paragraph. Three sentences max. Memorized. Uses concrete nouns, no buzzwords. Survives the bar test (could a stranger in a bar repeat it back without confusion?).

**The format:**
- I [verb that names the work] for [specific audience],
- specifically [specific outcome or capability].
- The unusual thing is [the differentiator that survives skepticism].

**Working draft (May 2026 — replace with your own):**
- I help small B2B teams turn AI tools from demos into operational systems.
- Specifically: skill packs, audits, and managed infrastructure for teams that have Claude or ChatGPT and don't yet have a way to make it stick.
- The unusual thing is I came up through hospitality and service desk work, so I read the failure modes that engineers usually ship past.

**Validation protocol:**
1. Say it to 5 people you know in the next 7 days. Watch their face. If they tilt their head or start asking clarifying questions, the statement is too abstract.
2. After each test, refine *one* word. Don't rewrite. Tighten.
3. Stop when 5 in a row immediately follow up with "oh, like for what kind of company?" — that's the signal it landed and they want more.

**Anti-pattern to avoid:** Don't add "10 years experience" or "self-taught" or any credential framing. Credentials in the positioning statement signal anxiety. The work is the credential.

### Project 2B — One External Proof Artifact (Weeks 3-4)

**The problem:** Private wins don't compound. Internal artifacts don't generate inbound. You can have shipped 40 things and be invisible.

**The deliverable:** One public, dated, durable artifact that demonstrates competence. URL exists. Searchable. Shareable.

**Acceptable forms:**
- A case study on your own site (preferred — Chris Butler suite is a candidate)
- A repo with a substantive README and a working demo
- An article with a specific technical claim and the evidence to back it
- A teardown of a real system with a specific recommendation

**Disqualified forms:**
- A LinkedIn post (too ephemeral, gone in 48 hours)
- A "thought leadership" piece that doesn't make a falsifiable claim
- Anything that requires the reader to take your word for something

**Quality bar (3 questions):**
1. Could a hiring manager link this from a Slack message and have the recipient understand the work in under 90 seconds?
2. Is there a specific number, system, or screenshot the reader can verify?
3. Does it make a claim that someone with industry experience could disagree with? (If everyone would nod, it's too generic.)

**Time budget:** 6-10 hours over the two weeks. If it's taking longer, scope is wrong.

### Project 2C — Stakeholder Map for the Market (Week 3)

**The problem:** "Stakeholder map" in the original plan meant internal political mapping. Reframe: the actual stakeholders are the people whose attention would change your outcomes. Those are mostly external.

**The deliverable:** A list of 7-12 specific people, with for each one:
- Name
- What they do that connects to your work
- The specific reason their attention would matter
- A first-touch action that's plausible (not "send cold email" — too generic)

**Categories to consider:**
- 2-3 journalists in your adjacent space (Lee Chong Ming style — operators-focused, AI-curious)
- 2-3 potential clients (people whose specific situation matches your case study)
- 2-3 peer builders (people doing similar work whose feedback would actually change your direction)
- 1-2 platform partners (e.g., Anthropic, Base44 if you build there — relevant only if you have a real reason)

**The first-touch standard:** A first touch is plausible if it would make sense even if no relationship existed. Sharing their work with a specific reaction. Asking a specific question their writing would equip them to answer. *Never* "I think we should connect."

**Cadence:** Touch one person per week. That's it. The stakeholder map is a long compounding instrument, not a sprint.

### Project 2D — Decline Protocol (Week 4)

**The problem:** Capability creates demand. Demand without a decline protocol creates burnout. Phase 1 protected your time *before* there was demand. Phase 2 protects it *during* demand.

**The deliverable:** A written, pre-decided rubric for what you say yes to and what you say no to. Three categories.

**Always yes (no thinking required):**
- Kinsley time
- Prior commitments to existing clients
- The smallest plausible step on the current top-priority artifact

**Always no (no thinking required):**
- Unpaid speaking gigs at companies you don't know
- Coffee chats from strangers asking generic advice
- Any new project that would push a current project past its committed deadline

**Conditional yes (think first):**
- Paid consulting from someone whose problem matches your case study
- Press requests where you've researched the outlet and the angle
- Collaborations where the partner's contribution is concrete and time-boxed

The point of the protocol isn't optimization — it's that *future you* doesn't have to make these calls in the moment, because *current you* already decided.

## Risk patterns to watch for

**Risk: Polishing the proof artifact for too long.** Mitigation: ship at 80%.

**Risk: Positioning statement gets clever instead of clear.** Mitigation: replace metaphors with the literal version.

**Risk: Stakeholder map becomes a fantasy list.** Mitigation: at least half should be people you could email today.

**Risk: Decline protocol exists on paper but breaks under pressure.** Mitigation: add violations to a lessons log.
