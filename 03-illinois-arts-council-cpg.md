## Project Description

*Construction, Not Recovery* is a book-length literary memoir structured as a letter to my daughter, Kinsley. It documents a period of cognitive collapse and reconstruction — addiction, traumatic brain injury caused by hypoxia, the loss of memory, processing, and identity — and the external systems, tools, and methods I built to continue functioning when internal systems failed.

The book's thesis is architectural: I did not recover in the conventional sense. I constructed a replacement system. This distinction matters. Recovery narratives follow a known arc — damage, crisis, healing, wisdom. This book follows a different arc. The damage is still present. The construction is ongoing. What changed is the infrastructure.

The memoir spans three periods: before (the bartender reading rooms and crowds, the quality control technician reading manufacturing processes, the self-taught engineer reading systems); during (the OD, the hospitalization, the cognitive testing, the revelation of a 99th-percentile processing speed paired with a 12th-percentile recall memory — an engine at full speed with no storage); and after (the tool-building, the external scaffolding, the diagnostic instinct that survived intact and became the foundation for everything constructed since).

**This is not a recovery story. A reader who finishes this book thinking "brave recovery story" has read the wrong book. The book will have failed.**

Key scenes and chapters include:
- O'Toole's, Milwaukee — the room-reading skill as origin, how a bartender diagnosing a crowd and a systems engineer diagnosing a codebase are the same act
- The card table (2015) — building a website for a life coach named Soaring Bear on a folding card table before I had a working desk, before I had a stable address, before any of this had a name. The method predates the injury. That is the structural finding of the book.
- The cognitive testing (2022) — sitting with neuropsychological data that explains the gap between capability and performance; understanding the architecture of my own failure
- The tool archaeology — reconstructing the history of compensation strategies I built without knowing I was building them
- The letter frame — every chapter is addressed to Kinsley. Not to explain what happened. To show her the method. So she has it.

The book has clear literary comparisons: Andrew Solomon's *The Noonday Demon* (examining mental states through research and personal experience simultaneously), Gabor Maté's *In the Realm of Hungry Ghosts* (addiction with structural rigor, without sentimentality), Susannah Cahalan's *Brain on Fire* (neurological disruption rendered as literary narrative).

## Artist Statement

I started building external systems to compensate for internal ones I could no longer trust. Note-taking systems. Checklists. Tools that externalized memory. Workflows that compensated for recall I didn't have. I did not know at the time that this was construction — I thought it was coping.

The memoir exists because I eventually needed to understand the difference.

I am a self-taught engineer, a father, and a former bartender who is writing a book from a service desk. I do not have an MFA. I have a 99th-percentile processing speed and a 12th-percentile recall memory and a daughter I want to give something more durable than a story about what happened to me.

The IAC Creative Projects Grant would support the editorial infrastructure this book requires. Literary nonfiction of this scope needs professional developmental editing — a reader who can hold the thesis against the material and tell me honestly when they diverge. I have the raw material. I have the method. I need the collaboration.

Illinois is where this book lives. The diagnostic instinct was trained in Milwaukee dive bars. The construction happened in Lake County. The Amen Clinics that produced the neuroimaging data is in Naperville. The book is an Illinois story, told from inside it.
