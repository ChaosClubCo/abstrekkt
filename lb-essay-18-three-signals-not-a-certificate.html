Three Signals, Not a Certificate

Not a certificate. Not tenure. Three things I actually watch for — in myself, and in anyone I'm assessing.

The client caught it before I did. That's the whole test, right there — not whether I've used the tool long enough, but whether that sentence is ever true.

Not credentials. Not tenure. Not how sophisticated the prompts look. Three things that actually show up in how someone works with AI output on the days something is at stake.

Three Things I Watch For

The first: catching wrong output before the client does. Not by reviewing everything at the same intensity — by knowing which twenty percent carries the risk and which eighty percent can move fast, because the domain knowledge already told me where to look. The test is simple and a little uncomfortable: when was the last time something wrong showed up because a client told me, not because I caught it first? If that's a regular occurrence, the judgment isn't there yet. That's a data point, not a verdict.

The second: using it to find what's wrong with the plan before executing it, not to produce the plan faster. Asking for the failure modes, the objection, the assumption that won't hold. The output I actually want is the one arguing against what I was already going to do. If AI only shows up in my workflow as a production tool, the pressure-test function is missing entirely.

The third: explaining why it was wrong, not just that it was. "This is wrong because it's applying a general rule to a situation with a specific exception." "This was right six months ago and the market moved." "This is technically accurate and ignores a pattern I've seen burn people in this exact spot." Noticing something's off is the beginning of the skill. Locating why, and whether the why changes anything, is the actual threshold.

None of the three measure AI literacy. None of them care how long I've used the tools or how many workflows I've built. All three require the kind of domain depth that only comes from having done the work wrong first, enough times, in enough different situations, that wrong registers before it has to be reasoned through.

The honest part

If none of the three describe how I currently work in a given domain, that's not a failure — it's a diagnostic. It means the gap between where I am and where the judgment lives is real, and specific, and usually narrower than it feels from the inside.

That's not a course. It's an accumulation. And it's the argument this whole run has been making since the first line: the judgment comes first. Speed, method, the context I bother to write down — all of it runs on top of whatever's already there.

If I've earned the judgment, I already use the tool differently. I catch things. I argue with it. I know the difference between a real error and a hallucination that just sounds sure of itself. If I'm not certain whether I do — that's the answer.
