The Ratio Inverts

The doing used to teach the judgment. Now the doing is automated, and the judgment is the only part left with no clean way to build it by accident.

For decades the path in knowledge work had the same shape everywhere: you start by doing the smaller version of the job. A junior associate drafts the brief a partner will rewrite. A new analyst runs the first pass a senior will correct. The doing is what teaches the judgment — quietly, through getting it wrong in front of someone who catches it before it costs anything. Eventually you get to direct instead of do.

AI is collapsing that ramp. Not the job — the path to judgment through execution. The doing is what's getting automated. What's left is knowing what to do with what gets done.

That's a different skill, and most people haven't had to build it on purpose, because execution used to build it for them by accident.

Ninety and Ten, Flipped

Old world: ninety percent of the time producing, ten percent reviewing. New world: the machine produces in seconds. Now what.

Now the ninety percent is spent figuring out whether what it produced is any good — why it failed, what it missed, where the confident-sounding paragraph is quietly wrong about something that matters.

That requires depth, not a better prompt. The person who can read an AI-drafted contract and catch the missing clause that creates the liability isn't a prompt engineer. That's someone who kept sharpening the one thing a model can't fake: judgment built from actual cases, not tokens.

A tool changes every six months. The skill of reading a system doesn't.

Which is why the job disappearing was never the part that worried me. The part that worries me is the people who never built the judgment layer, because they were always told the execution layer was the point — and the execution layer is exactly what's going away first.

What It Actually Breaks

For someone already senior, this is mostly a role expansion. For someone starting out, it's closer to a crisis: the grunt work that used to build the intuition is disappearing before the intuition gets built. There's no clean fix for that yet. Some places are trying to compress it — feeding trainees case after synthetic case, trying to manufacture years of pattern exposure in months. Whether that produces the same judgment as doing the actual work is a real open question. Nobody's proven it either way.

The hiring question is changing underneath people who haven't noticed yet. "Can they use the tool" stopped being interesting the day the tool got easy enough for everyone to use. The question underneath it — can they tell when the tool is wrong — was always the real one. It just used to be optional.
