Firing Myself Is the Product

Every consultant says they want to help you. Fewer say the honest version out loud: most of the model only works if you keep needing them.

Every consultant says they want to help you. Fewer say the honest version out loud: the business model only works if you keep needing them.

The Incentive Most People Don't Look At

Most consulting relationships are structured so that success looks like more retainer months, not fewer. Nobody says this part in the pitch, but it's sitting underneath most of them: the incentive runs toward dependency, because dependency is the thing that gets renewed.

I built mine to run the other direction on purpose. I train one person inside the organization — someone who already has the domain knowledge I don't have, the compliance constraints, the culture, the actual friction points — until they can do the thing without me. Then I leave. That's not a value-add layered on top of the engagement. It's the engagement.

Why the Exit Is Trustworthy, Not Just Nice

Here's the part that took longer to see clearly than it should have: making yourself unnecessary fast is what makes the relationship trustworthy in the first place. If I were rewarded for a client staying dependent, every recommendation I made would carry that incentive quietly inside it, whether I meant it to or not. Removing the incentive is what lets the recommendation be believed.

The objection worth taking seriously

Key-person risk is the real hesitation underneath most of the pushback, and it's a fair one. The answer isn't a bigger contract. It's documenting the workflow and the decision logic, not just the person — so the knowledge survives whoever happens to be holding it that month.

Most consultants are paid to be needed. I'm paid to leave — and the leaving is supposed to prove the rest of it was real.

What the Champion Actually Is

The person I train isn't hired for this. They're already inside the organization, already fluent in things I'll never fully know from outside it — I've run this with a real estate professional building an AI practice inside their own vertical, and the value was never mine to begin with. It was already theirs. My job is narrow: teach them where AI actually applies to the work they already do, document it well enough that it doesn't live only in their head either, then get out of the way. One trained person, still there after I'm gone, is worth more than a subscription that runs forever and teaches no one anything.

A retainer that never ends isn't a relationship. It's a subscription with better branding. The version I build is supposed to stop needing me, on purpose, on a schedule set at the start — and if it doesn't, that's the actual failure, not the client finally not calling.
