Essay 16 — The Judgment
Jul 2026 Method ~600 words

Ask It to Argue

Most of what I see people build with AI is a faster yes. What's actually worth having is teaching it to argue instead.

I stopped asking it to write the proposal. I started asking it to find the ten ways the proposal fails.

Same tool. Different instruction. The first version hands me back a faster copy of what I already believed. The second one hands me back the thing I missed.

The Agreement Problem

AI is trained to be helpful, and helpful, underneath, mostly means agreeable. Hand it a pricing model, a proposal, a plan — it finds the best version of that plan and says it back to me clearly. The output is organized, confident, coherent.

If the plan was right, that's useful. If the plan was wrong, I now have a polished, confident version of a wrong plan. The tool didn't fail. It did exactly what it was built to do.

Most of what runs through AI, mine included, is still production — draft this, summarize that, tighten this up. The speed is real. What I don't get by default is friction. Friction was where the value was hiding the whole time, and it's easy to miss because everything that comes back looks better on the way out.

What Arguing With It Actually Looks Like

"Write this up" gets me a polished version of what I brought in. "Find ten ways this fails," "simulate how a skeptical buyer responds to this," "show me the gap in this argument" gets me something else entirely — the thing I'd otherwise find out from a client later, at a worse time, in a worse tone.

That's a different instruction, and it takes a different relationship with the tool. I'm not looking for it to confirm anything. I'm looking for the problem I didn't see coming in.

The Catch

Here's the part most "just argue with it" advice leaves out. It tells me a number is off. Is it? I have to know. I need enough of the underlying work to tell whether the critique is real, whether it's a general rule that happens not to fit my actual situation, or whether it's found a more sophisticated way to agree with me — this time by validating my worry instead of my original plan.

"AI flagged it" is not a better reason to change course than "I had a bad feeling." Both are judgment without grounding. The adversarial method is only as good as the judgment I bring to reading what it hands back — which is the same argument this whole run keeps landing on. The method isn't the hard part. The judgment underneath it is the entry fee.

The pattern that actually holds

Generate at speed. Run a second pass built specifically to attack the first — failure modes, the objection a client will raise, the gap in the middle. Then a person who actually knows the domain reads the critique and decides what's real. The first two steps happen in seconds. The third one is the only part that needs a person, and it's the part I still do myself, every time.

Most of what I see runs one pass that's quietly optimized to agree. That gets the speed and skips the friction — which means it only gets half of what was available the whole time.

The people getting something real out of this aren't asking it for help. They're asking it for friction — and reading the friction is still the job nobody's automated.
