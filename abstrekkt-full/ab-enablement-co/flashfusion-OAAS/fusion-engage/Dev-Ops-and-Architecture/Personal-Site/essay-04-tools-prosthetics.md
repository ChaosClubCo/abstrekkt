# The Prosthetics
*kylerosebrook.com — personal essay draft*

---

The first system I built wasn't sophisticated. It was embarrassing.

A text file. Actually, several text files — one for each major area of my life that I kept losing track of. What I was supposed to do today. What I'd told someone last week. What the current state of a given project was, because I could no longer trust that I'd remember the current state of a given project. The files grew. I started adding dates. Then I started adding context, because dates without context turned out to be useless — a timestamp is not a memory. I started capturing not just what but why and how and what comes next, because the thing I was discovering was that I couldn't trust any of it to be there when I reached for it.

This was not how I'd operated before. Before, memory was air. You don't think about air. It's just there, doing its job, and you use it without noticing it's being used. I remembered drink orders. I remembered names and faces and what people had told me weeks ago. I remembered where I was in multi-threaded conversations. I remembered what I'd been doing when I got interrupted and could pick it back up without a trace of where I'd been. The retrieval mechanism worked. I didn't have a retrieval mechanism — I had memory.

Then I didn't.

Not all at once. This is another thing the popular narrative gets wrong — it implies a clear before and after, a specific moment when the switch flipped, a date you can point to and say *this is when it changed.* The reality is more like a slow drift you don't notice until you're significantly off course. One day I'd blank on something I should have known. Then it was more frequent. Then I started noticing patterns in what I was losing. The brand-new information was unreliable. Recent context was unreliable. Sequences of events were unreliable. Long-term consolidated memory — the stuff from years ago, the deep past — was sometimes fine and sometimes not.

The clinical word for this is "memory impairment." The lived experience of it is: you become an unreliable narrator of your own life.

The text files were embarrassing because they were visible evidence of a problem I wasn't fully ready to name. Every time I added an entry, I was acknowledging that the organic version of whatever that entry represented was unavailable. I was building workarounds. And the psychological weight of building workarounds, when you don't yet understand that workarounds can be the actual solution rather than the inadequate substitute, is heavy in a specific way that's hard to describe accurately.

It feels like defeat. For a while. Until it doesn't.

The shift — and I'm going to be careful here, because I don't entirely trust my memory of when it happened — was somewhere around when I started treating the systems as the thing, rather than as the substitute for the thing. When I stopped thinking of external documentation as the sad replacement for the internal recall I didn't have, and started thinking of it as a different architecture that had its own properties and in some ways better ones.

External documentation doesn't forget. It doesn't degrade over a bad week or reconstruct incorrectly after stress. It doesn't confabulate — it doesn't fill in gaps with plausible fiction and present the result as fact, which is what damaged memory does and what makes it particularly treacherous. The document just holds what you put in it, neutrally, indefinitely. That's not worse than organic memory. It's differently reliable, with a specific failure mode (you have to actually write it down) that is far more manageable than the failure mode of organic memory (it can lie to you without announcing that it's lying).

I stopped building workarounds. I started building infrastructure.

The distinction matters. Infrastructure is what you build when you intend to live in it. It's designed with load in mind, with growth in mind, with the understanding that other things will depend on it. Workarounds are for temporary conditions you expect to resolve. Infrastructure is for permanent conditions you're designing around.

The tool count grew because the problem was layered. Memory was one layer. Attention was another — not the O'Toole's dual-track state, which was functional, but the kind of attentional dysregulation that follows significant cognitive disruption, where the processor runs fine but the scheduler is compromised. Task management needed external structure. Context-switching needed protocols. Knowledge management needed a system — not a folder, a system, with taxonomy and retrieval and the ability to find something three months later when you need it. Each problem got a tool. The tools accumulated.

People ask about the tool count and I watch their faces cycle through impressed and then skeptical and then something that looks like concern. You have that many tools running? What does that look like? Isn't that overwhelming?

It's not overwhelming. It's just what cognition looks like when you've had to make the implicit explicit. Most people are running equivalent complexity — equal amounts of memory load, attention management, context switching, knowledge retrieval. They're running it natively, on hardware that handles it without needing external scaffolding. I'm running it with the scaffolding showing.

Neither version is inherently better. They have different properties. Native cognition is faster, more fluid, less friction-heavy. Externalized cognition is more auditable, more shareable, more robust to bad days and disrupted sleep and stress responses. When my native hardware is having a rough week — and it does have rough weeks — the external systems hold. The documentation is still there. The processes are still documented. The infrastructure doesn't care what kind of morning I had.

What I've built over the years is not a productivity system. That word gets used about tool stacks and I understand why — it's the available vocabulary — but it misses the point. Productivity implies optimization. What I built was survivability. The goal wasn't to do more. The goal was to not lose track of who I was and what I was doing and why.

The tools are prosthetics. That's the honest word for them. Prosthetics are not inferior to the thing they replace — they're a different solution to the same underlying problem of function. A well-designed prosthetic can outperform a damaged original in specific conditions. It's certainly preferable to the alternative, which is attempting to operate with a missing function and hoping no one notices.

The first text file was embarrassing. Every system I've built since has been less embarrassing than the one before it, not because the problem is resolving but because my relationship to the problem changed. I stopped being ashamed of the gap between what my memory can do and what I need it to do. The gap is just a spec. Here's the required function. Here's what native provides. Here's the delta. Build for the delta.

What I have now is functional in ways the pre-break version wasn't — different ways, with different failure modes, but genuinely functional. I can work at a level of complexity that should be inaccessible given what the neurocognitive assessments show about memory and recall. I can because the architecture compensates.

The book I'm writing is, among other things, an inventory of that architecture. How it was built. What it replaced. What it can and can't do. What it cost to build it and what it would have cost not to.

The first entry is a text file from however many years ago, now probably long deleted, that said something like: *things I need to remember today.* Followed by the list.

That was the beginning of the infrastructure.

I didn't know it at the time. I was just trying not to lose the thread.

*Kyle Rosebrook is a self-taught engineer and founder. "Construction, Not Recovery" is a literary memoir in progress about rebuilding cognitive function through external systems.*
