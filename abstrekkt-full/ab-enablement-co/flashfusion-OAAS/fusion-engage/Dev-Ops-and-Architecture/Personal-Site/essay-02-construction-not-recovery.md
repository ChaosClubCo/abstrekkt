# The Problem With the Word "Recovery"
*kylerosebrook.com — personal essay draft*

---

Nobody questions the framing. That's the first thing.

Recovery is everywhere — in the language of medicine, in the structure of support groups, in the way people talk about anyone who's been through something hard and come out the other side. *You're recovering. How's your recovery going? What a recovery.* It's presented as obviously correct, the way certain assumptions get presented when no one involved has stopped to ask whether they're true.

I want to ask.

Recovery, as a concept, assumes a baseline. A prior state that functioned, that has been disrupted, that the process is working to restore. It assumes the goal is return. Get back to who you were. Get back to how you functioned. Get back.

That model works if what you lost is recoverable. If the disruption was external and temporary — a broken leg heals, a business recovers from a bad quarter, an athlete rehabilitates an injury and returns to play. The structure holds because the target state still exists. You know where you're trying to get back to.

It doesn't work if the disruption changed the hardware.

I've spent a long time trying to explain the difference between "I'm working on myself" and "I'm building something that didn't exist before." They feel similar from the outside — effort, intention, visible change over time. From the inside, they're categorically different. One is oriented toward a prior state. The other is oriented toward a possible future state that has no precedent. One is navigation with a known destination. The other is construction with a blueprint you're drawing as you go.

The distinction isn't academic for me. It's structural. If I frame what I'm doing as recovery, I'm operating with a target that doesn't exist anymore. The brain I'm trying to get back to isn't available. The memory function that made the previous version of me work — the thing that let me hold a full shift in my head, that let me track complex social situations in real time without external scaffolding — that's not coming back in the form it existed before. That's not a failure of effort or willingness or the right therapeutic approach. That's just what is.

Recovery, as a frame, makes that a problem. Makes the gap between current state and prior state a deficit to be addressed. Makes the distance between who you were and who you are now a measure of how much you still have left to do.

Construction doesn't work that way.

Construction starts with an honest material assessment. What do I have? What are its properties? What can I build with it? The prior state isn't the target — it's one input among several, useful insofar as it tells you something about the material you're working with. You're not trying to recreate the original structure. You're trying to build something functional from what's available, using techniques appropriate to the actual situation rather than the idealized prior one.

The tool stacks I run — the external systems that function as prosthetic memory, the documentation practices that turn unreliable internal recall into reliable external record, the structured approaches to tasks that organic cognitive function used to handle automatically — these aren't symptoms of incomplete recovery. They're infrastructure. They're what a constructed self looks like when the building materials are specific and honest about their own properties.

A recovered self would be trying to stop needing them. A constructed self never needed to start with — it built them in because they're load-bearing.

Here's what the recovery frame actually costs people, and I want to be specific about this because I think it's underappreciated: it creates an implicit ceiling. If the goal is return, then every adaptation you make is a workaround. Every compensatory strategy is a sign you haven't gotten there yet. Every system you build to manage a limitation is evidence of the limitation rather than evidence of the construction. The whole frame pathologizes adaptation. It says: the workaround is not the real you, the real you is the one who doesn't need workarounds, keep working toward the real you.

This is, to use a technical term, backwards.

The workarounds are not evidence of the problem. The workarounds are the solution. They're the actual product of the actual work. A person who has rebuilt cognitive function using external systems is not less functional than a person running equivalent function natively — they're differently functional, and in some specific ways more robust, because they've had to make explicit what most people keep implicit. Every process documented is a process that can be audited, improved, shared. Every system externalized is a system that doesn't collapse with a bad night's sleep.

I'm not arguing that cognitive disruption is good. I'm arguing that the thing you build in response to it can be genuinely better in specific ways than what was there before, and that the recovery frame makes that impossible to see because it's always measuring current state against prior state rather than against possible future states.

The prior state is not the benchmark. It's just data.

What I've found — and I say "found" carefully because I don't fully trust my memory of the process of finding it — is that the constructed self has access to some things the prior self didn't. Not more memory. The memory's worse, and probably always will be. But a certain kind of clarity that comes from having had to make the implicit explicit. A relationship with systems and structure that most people don't develop because they don't have to. A specific tolerance for uncertainty that comes from having operated under radical uncertainty for an extended period and discovered that it doesn't kill you.

These aren't consolation prizes. They're not the "hidden gifts of struggle" reframe that recovery narratives love so much. They're just real things that are present in the current configuration and were not present in the prior one. They exist because of the disruption, not despite it. They couldn't have been built any other way.

The book I'm writing is called "Construction, Not Recovery." Not as a slogan. As a literal description of what happened. Something broke. I didn't fix it. I built around it, through it, with it, using whatever was available — including the break itself as a structural element. The result is not the original. It's also not a compromise.

It's what I built.

The recovery frame would have me measuring the distance between what I have now and what I had before. The construction frame asks a different question: given what I have now, what can I make?

That question has a much better answer.

*Kyle Rosebrook is a self-taught engineer and founder. "Construction, Not Recovery" is a literary memoir in progress about rebuilding agency after cognitive disruption.*
