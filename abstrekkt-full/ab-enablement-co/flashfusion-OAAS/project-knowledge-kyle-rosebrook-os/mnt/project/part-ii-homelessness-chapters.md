# CHAPTER [X]: THE DISCHARGE

The thing nobody tells you is that leaving treatment is its own kind of crash.

Not the dramatic kind. Not withdrawal or relapse or a phone call from someone saying something bad happened. Just the specific thud of a door closing behind you at a time you did not choose, and suddenly the day has no structure and nobody is tracking what you eat or when you sleep, and the clipboard that's been your external nervous system for however many weeks is staying behind with the program.

I don't remember the exact date I left Salvation Army [VERIFY: approximate date/season]. I remember the temperature, which means it was probably [VERIFY: season — cold? warm?]. I remember the weight of the bag. Everything I owned was in it, which is a fact that should have carried more emotional weight than it did. It mostly just felt logistically inconvenient. You move slower when you're carrying everything.

The discharge paperwork has a section for where you're going. There's a line. The assumption built into the form is that you have an answer. I had an answer. I wrote something down. [VERIFY: what did he write? Family? A friend? Made something up?] The answer I wrote was not the situation I was walking into, which makes it either a lie or optimism depending on what you think I was capable of at the time.

I was capable of reading the room. That had not left me. If anything, the combination of addiction and whatever treatment does to the brain's fight-or-flight processing had turned the diagnostic instinct up higher than it had ever been in the bar. I could read a face from across a parking lot. I could tell in thirty seconds if a space was safe. That skill was, at this point, almost entirely useless. The rooms I needed to read were: whose couch this was, whether they meant it when they said I could stay, and how long I had before the answer changed.

The meetings were supposed to be the bridge. Ninety meetings in ninety days, or something close to it. The meetings are where you build the network that replaces the using network, which is sound in theory. In practice, the meetings were in church basements and community rooms, and after the meeting you shook hands and went back to wherever you came from, and I was coming from the doorway between the church and the parking lot, trying to calculate who I could ask for a couch without it meaning something they'd hold over me later.

The first couch wasn't a terrible couch. [VERIFY: whose couch? Anyone Kyle can name?] The arrangement was informal — an understanding without a conversation. I was there, I was quiet, I stayed out of the way. The unspoken transaction was: I don't make your life harder and you don't make me leave. That's not gratitude and it's not obligation. It's a lease with no paperwork, renewable night by night.

I knew how long I had because I was reading the room constantly. The posture in the kitchen. Whether the TV went off before or after I went to sleep. How many times someone cleared their throat in a hallway I was in. I had read drunks and regulars and people who were about to get cut off for twelve years. I could read a living room.

The problem was that reading the room correctly didn't change anything about the room. Knowing that the welcome was wearing thin didn't give me somewhere else to go. The diagnostic instinct, which had always been followed by action — cut someone off, redirect a conversation, move someone out of a situation — had nothing to act on. I was just sitting there with accurate information and nowhere to put it.

That's the part the pamphlets don't cover.

# CHAPTER [X+1]: THE COUCH ECONOMY

At some point in that six months, someone started charging me for the couch.

I don't mean they handed me an invoice. I mean there was a conversation, and at the end of it there was a number, and the couch became something I was paying for. [VERIFY: how much? Cash? Weekly?] The number was small enough to feel like a favor and large enough to be a transaction. I paid it. Paying felt better than not paying, which is the logic of someone who needs the arrangement to mean something different than what it actually means.

The couch-to-pay-for is a specific step. It comes after the free couch, which is charity, and before the room, which is something like stability. It's the middle category that doesn't have a name. You're not homeless if you're paying rent, even couch rent, even if what you're renting is eighty percent of a sectional in someone's living room. The reclassification matters more than the furniture.

I was a good tenant. This is not a sentence I say with pride — it's just accurate. I didn't bring chaos into spaces that weren't mine. I read the room, I knew the rules even when no one stated them, I disappeared when disappearing was required. If you needed the couch on a Tuesday for some reason, I was not on the couch on Tuesday. If someone needed the bathroom first in the morning, I was already done with the bathroom. [VERIFY: does this ring true?]

The rule I could not solve was the one about trajectory. Every couch has a horizon. The arrangement that works today works because it hasn't worn out yet, and it will wear out, and the only question is whether you have something lined up before it does or whether you end up doing the math on other options at eleven at night on a Thursday.

I did that math several times.

The room came after the couch. [VERIFY: what kind of room? Boarding house? Someone's spare bedroom? Basement?] Smaller space, clearer terms. Four walls you can close a door inside, which is not nothing. The door changes your nervous system in a way that's hard to explain unless you've spent time without one. A door is information — it signals that the space on the other side of it is temporarily yours, that you don't have to be performing not-being-a-problem at all hours.

My brain was still running the couch calculations in the room. Threat-scanning a space that was technically safe because the safe space was the same hardware as the unsafe one. [VERIFY: did the hypervigilance feel this persistent?] The diagnostic instinct doesn't have a rest mode. You can't tell it the situation has changed and expect it to update gracefully.

The hotel rooms came later, and they came in bursts. [VERIFY: how did hotel rooms get funded? Cash from what source?] A hotel room is a couch that doesn't require you to read anyone else. That was worth whatever it cost. No host whose mood I had to track from the parking lot, no kitchen I was navigating around someone else's schedule. Just a key card and a door that dead-bolted and a TV I could leave on while I slept.

The problem with hotel rooms is the math. A hotel room is thirty or sixty or eighty dollars a night [VERIFY: price range], which is sustainable for however many nights you can sustain it and then it isn't. I was sustainable for a few nights at a stretch. Then not sustainable. Then sustainable again when something came through. The rhythm of it was not a rhythm. It was a sequence with irregular spacing, which is different.

In between the hotel rooms and the couch and the room, there were variations I won't catalog because the memoir doesn't need an inventory. What it needs is the texture, and the texture was this: every morning was a calculation about whether the arrangement I had would still be the arrangement by evening. Six months of that calculation, compounding. The diagnostic instinct running its checks on every space I was in. Accurate readings. Nothing to do with the accuracy.

There's a version of this story where I say that being that careful for that long built something in me. Some resilience, some skill. I don't think that's true. I think it's the kind of story people tell about hard periods to make the hard period mean something. What it actually built was a very efficient threat-scanning operation in a brain that already had threat-scanning as its primary output. That was not an asset I needed more of.

What I needed was for Pete to show up.

# CHAPTER [X+2]: PETE AT SEVEN

I don't remember which day of the week it was. I remember it was around seven in the evening [VERIFY: accuracy of the time — does this ring true?], which is a specific kind of time. Not late enough to be night, not early enough to be afternoon. The hour where you can't tell if something is about to happen or if nothing is going to happen and the day is just going to turn into the next one, same as the last one.

Pete had been gone for a while. [VERIFY: how long? Days? Weeks?] The specific geometry of owing someone something and then being unreachable is familiar to anyone who's run that economy. The person who owes disappears not because they've forgotten the debt — they remember the debt exactly, that's why they're disappeared — but because disappearing is cheaper than having the conversation. [VERIFY: was Pete a using friend? Someone from that circle?]

It was his turn to buy. [VERIFY: is this accurate? Or is this a shorthand Kyle used?] That is a sentence with a very specific meaning in a very specific context and I'm not going to over-explain it. What it means practically is that the cycle had gotten to his turn, and when it was your turn you showed up with what you were supposed to show up with, and Pete had not been showing up.

I wasn't waiting for Pete the way you wait for something you've planned. I was just existing in whatever space I was in [VERIFY: hotel room? room? couch situation at this point?], and at some point there was a knock, or a text, or a call [VERIFY: how did Pete announce himself?], and Pete was there. At seven. In the specific way that someone who has been unavailable is suddenly available, which is the way of someone who has now run out of reasons to stay unavailable.

He looked like what he was [VERIFY: was Pete visibly using? What did he look like?]. I probably looked like what I was. Neither of us commented on that.

The arrival of Pete did not fix anything. I want to be clear about that because this is the kind of moment that gets written as a pivot — the night something changed, the person who showed up that mattered. Pete was not that. Pete was a specific person in a specific economy who showed up when it was his turn, six months into a stretch where I was reading every room for exits and none of the rooms were that different from each other.

But I remember the time. Seven o'clock. That detail stayed when a lot of other details from that stretch didn't.

Memory keeps what it keeps for reasons I can't fully audit. I've stopped trying to audit the reasons and started just recording the data. The data is: seven PM, Pete at the door, his turn to buy. The context around it — what came immediately before, what the space looked like, what I said when I opened the door — that's where the record gets thin. [VERIFY: what does Kyle actually remember about this night beyond the time and Pete's arrival?]

What I know is that the six months ended around then. Not that night exactly, maybe, but near it. The couch progression was coming to a close, the hotel room math was shifting. [VERIFY: what actually ended the homelessness period — Pete's arrival directly? Or was it coincident with something else?] Something in the sequence changed.

This is the part where I'm supposed to tell you that was the night I decided things were going to be different. That I looked at Pete at the door and saw my reflection and made a choice. That the seven PM arrival was a catalyst.

It wasn't.

The addiction ran for a while longer. The construction hadn't started yet. What Pete's arrival marks — what it marks in the way a timestamp marks something without explaining it — is the end of the chapter where I was calculating the couch situation every morning. Not because I arrived somewhere. Because that particular arrangement wound down and a different one started.

The brain was still the same brain. Processing at a speed that was probably too high for what I was using it on. Memory that wasn't keeping the things I needed it to keep. The diagnostic instinct reading rooms that didn't require diagnosing.

Pete was there. It was seven.

The next part of the story is not better. But it's a different chapter.
