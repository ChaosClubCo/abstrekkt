How Do You Know When You've Earned It?

Not credentials. Not the tools you're running. Not how long you've been in the industry. Three observable behaviors tell you whether the judgment is actually there.

January 2027 FlashFusion 4 min read

The question isn't whether you've used AI long enough. The question is whether you use it differently than someone who hasn't earned the judgment. Three signals tell you.

Not credentials. Not tenure. Not the complexity of your prompts or the number of workflows you've automated. Observable behaviors, things that show up in how you actually work with AI output on the days when something is at stake.

01
You catch wrong output before your client does
Not by reviewing everything at the same level, by knowing which outputs carry risk. The judgment tells you which 20% needs adversarial scrutiny and why. The other 80% can move fast. The review is selective, not exhaustive, because the domain expertise tells you where to look.
The test
When was the last time AI produced something wrong and your first signal was your client telling you, not your own review catching it? If that happens regularly, the judgment isn't there yet. That's a data point, not a verdict.
02
You use AI to find what's wrong with your plan before you execute it
Not to produce the plan faster. To pressure-test it before it reaches a client. You're asking for the failure modes, the objections, the assumption that doesn't hold under scrutiny. The output you care most about is the one that argues against what you were going to do anyway.
The test
When was the last time you asked AI to argue against something you were planning to do, before you did it? If AI is exclusively a production tool in your workflow, the pressure-test function is missing.
03
You can explain why the AI was wrong, not just that it was
"This output is wrong because it's applying general logic to a situation with a specific exception." "This recommendation was correct six months ago but the market moved." "This is technically accurate but ignores the liability pattern we've seen in this sector." You can trace the error to the gap. You're not just noticing, you're locating.
The test
"Something seems wrong" is the beginning of the skill. "Here's what's wrong and here's why it matters in this specific case" is the threshold. The AI can flag that it might be wrong. Only your expertise can say why, and whether the why changes anything.

What all three signals have in common: none of them measure AI literacy. None of them care how long you've used the tools, how sophisticated your prompts are, or how many workflows you've built. All three require domain depth, the kind that accumulates from having done the work wrong first, enough times, in enough different contexts, that wrong output registers before it has to be reasoned through.

That's not a course. It's not a certification. It's an accumulation. And it's the thing this series has been arguing for since the first post: the judgment comes first. Speed, method, context strategy, all of it runs on top.

If you've earned the judgment, you already use AI differently. You catch things. You pressure-test. You know the difference between a real AI error and a sophisticated-sounding hallucination. If you're not sure whether you do, that's the answer.
The honest note

If none of these three signals describe how you currently work, that's not a failure, it's a diagnostic. The gap between where the judgment is and where these signals live is exactly what structured AI enablement addresses. Not by skipping the accumulation, but by accelerating the feedback loops, building practice around AI output review, and identifying where in your organization the judgment already exists and where it still needs to develop. Most organizations have more of it than they think, and more gaps than they'd like. The audit is where that picture gets clear.
