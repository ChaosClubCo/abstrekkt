If You've Earned the Judgment  ·  02 of 05

The Ratio Inverts

Your job used to be producing the output. The machine does that now. Your job is what happens next, and most people haven't practiced it.

November 2026 FlashFusion 5 min read

Your job used to be producing the output. The machine does that now. Your job is what happens next, and most people haven't practiced it.

That's not a criticism of your team. It's a structural description of what happened. Knowledge work built its practitioners by keeping them in the producing role long enough that judgment accumulated as a byproduct. That mechanism just got disrupted at scale, and most organizations haven't registered what they lost when it went.

How expertise used to build itself

Knowledge-intensive roles operated on a consistent pattern for decades: you produced, your output got reviewed, you revised, you produced again. The cycle was slow and sometimes humiliating and deeply effective.

A junior analyst produced the first draft. A senior analyst caught what was wrong and explained why. The junior analyst revised. Over three to five years of that cycle, the junior had internalized enough of the senior's pattern recognition to start catching their own errors, and eventually someone else's.

Judgment didn't get taught directly. It accumulated through the friction of producing badly and having that badness identified in specific terms. The job was the training program. Nobody designed it that way. It emerged from the constraint that producing required skill, time, and revision, and failure was visible enough to learn from.

What AI changed

The producing now takes seconds.

Not for every task. Not for the highest-stakes work that requires rare domain depth. But for the category of work that used to occupy the majority of a junior knowledge worker's day, drafting, summarizing, structuring, first-pass analysis, the time cost has collapsed.

Old ratio
Producing
90%
Reviewing
10%
The 90% was also the training ground. Judgment built up inside it.
New ratio
Producing
10%
Evaluating
90%
Is this right? What did it miss? What's confidently wrong? That's the work now.

The domain knowledge requirement didn't decrease when AI entered the workflow. It intensified. Before AI, a wrong draft was usually visible, incomplete, missing something a reviewer could identify on sight. Now the wrong draft is polished, confident, and wrong in a specific contextual way that only someone with deep domain knowledge can detect.

The person who can read the AI-generated contract and spot the missing clause that creates a liability? That's not a prompting skill. That's a lawyer who kept sharpening the thing AI can't replicate: judgment accumulated from cases, not from documents about cases. The bar for evaluating AI output is higher than the bar for producing manually, because the errors no longer come flagged with visible incompleteness. They come wrapped in competence.

The junior problem

This is where most analysis of the AI shift either stops or goes vague. It's worth being direct about it.

The mechanism that used to build judgment was the production cycle, produce, fail, revise, repeat, accumulate pattern recognition over years. AI is collapsing that ramp. Not the job. The path to judgment through execution. The doing is getting automated. What's left is the knowing-what-to-do-with-what-gets-done.

Junior people entering knowledge-intensive fields now have to develop judgment without the experience that used to create it. They're being asked to evaluate outputs in domains they don't have the depth to evaluate. The work that would have built that depth over three to five years is no longer their job.

Open problem, no clean answer yet

Some organizations are building synthetic experience: structured audit tasks, edge-case libraries, outputs specifically designed to contain the kinds of errors junior reviewers need to learn to catch. It's early work. Most organizations haven't figured it out. The ones who say they have are usually describing a training program and calling it a solution. The honest position is that this is unsolved, and ignoring it doesn't close it, it just means the gap surfaces later, when AI output has scaled and the cost of bad evaluation has become expensive.

This isn't a reason to avoid AI adoption. It's a reason to build the evaluation capability alongside the deployment, not after the deployment has already scaled.

The hiring question is backward

If evaluation is now the primary skill, if 90% of the work is catching what AI got wrong rather than producing in the first place, then the hiring question has to invert with the ratio.

Common question
"Can this person use AI tools?"
Measures production speed. Was roughly right when production was 90% of the work. Actively misleading now.
Right question
"Can this person evaluate AI output in this domain, under pressure, at the speed the machine generates it?"
Measures judgment. Different bar. Harder to assess. The one that determines whether the AI investment pays off.

The candidate who writes elegant prompts but can't articulate why an output is wrong is more dangerous in a high-stakes role than the candidate who prompts slowly and catches failures quickly. Most job descriptions haven't caught up to this. Most screening processes are still optimizing for the old ratio, selecting for production speed in a world where production speed is the easy part.

The organizations that figure this out first will hire differently, develop differently, and retain differently than the ones still running the old playbook. That advantage compounds quietly until it doesn't.
