The Grand Explanation
How one scan connects everything
On July 7, 2022, a 36-year-old bartender named Kyle Rosebrook sat in a dimly lit room at Amen Clinics Chicago while a radioactive tracer mapped the blood flow in his brain. Four days later, he came back for a second scan at rest. He was there because his mother, Phillis, had found the clinic. His chief complaint was two words:
"Addiction, depression."
What the scan revealed was a map of damage — decreased perfusion across the temporal lobes, prefrontal cortex, parietal cortex, occipital lobes, and cerebellum. Scalloping on the surface consistent with toxic exposure and oxygen deprivation. A thalamus running severely hot. The clinical summary:
evidence of past brain injury and encephalopathic process.
The cause was not a car accident. Not a fall. Not a blow to the head. Kyle's brain was injured by his own medications — a Xanax and Ambien overdose in the summer of 2020 that deprived his brain of oxygen. The addiction didn't just coexist with the brain injury.
The addiction caused the brain injury. And then the brain injury made escape harder
, because the prefrontal cortex that handles impulse control was now running at reduced capacity. A recursive trap.
The memoir's thesis in one sentence: Recovery assumes returning to a previous state. Kyle can't return — the hardware is changed. So he built a new architecture from non-standard materials, using a brain that processes at the 99th percentile but recalls at the 12th.
This document maps how every construction in Kyle's life — the 240+ tools, the engineering career, Chaos Club Co., the memoir itself — traces back to what those four images showed. Not as metaphor. As engineering response to a diagnosed system failure.
Among the living dead, I was king.
— Kyle, on being the model resident at Roger's Behavioral Health, summer 2020

Kyle thought the 99th/12th split meant his brain was broken across the board. It means the opposite.
The processing engine is elite. The storage system is devastated.
His brain catches everything — patterns, systems, room dynamics, code architecture — and then can't hold it. The information arrives at full speed and evaporates.
This is why the 240+ tools work. He's not compensating for being slow.
He's building an external hard drive for a processor that runs too fast for its own memory.
The obsessive documentation, the systematic approaches, the Claude projects, the Notion databases, the n8n workflows — all of it is prosthetic recall for a brain that can process at the 99th percentile but store at the 12th.
