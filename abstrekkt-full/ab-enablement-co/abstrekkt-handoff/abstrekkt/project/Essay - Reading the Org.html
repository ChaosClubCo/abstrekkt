Reading the Org

Same questions. Different rooms.

October 2026 Personal essay 5 min read

I ask three questions before I quote any engagement. None of them are about tools.

The first: Is this happening top-down or bottom-up, and who actually owns the outcome? The second: Where's the gap between what leadership thinks AI is doing and what's happening on the floor? The third: Who decides what good output looks like? Who catches it when it's wrong?

I've been asking versions of these for longer than the consulting work has existed. At the bar, I was asking who owned the room and who was letting the room own them. On the QC floor, I was reading where the process was about to fail rather than where it was currently failing. In the ticket queue, I was reading through the description to the state underneath. The questions haven't changed. The rooms have.

· · ·

Reading an organization's AI readiness is the same diagnostic move at a different scale.

The ownership question is the bar question, not which customer is about to become a problem, but who in this organization is responsible for whether this works. Most have tools. Almost none have anyone whose actual job is to know whether the tools are running correctly and what to do when they aren't. The ownership vacuum shows up in the silence when I ask. The more confident the answer, the more likely someone approved a deployment and called it ownership.

The reality gap question is the floor question, where does what the process reports diverge from what the process is actually producing? Organizations report AI adoption. They report training sessions and use cases and subscriptions. What's actually running is almost always different, usually less, and occasionally more dangerous than what's been reported. The defect in the output isn't always visible at the reporting layer. That's why you have to read through it.

The accountability question is the ticket queue question, not what the user noticed, but who is responsible for the output the system is producing. Who catches the wrong answer before it reaches the client? Who owns the failure mode nobody designed for? In the ticket queue, silence on this question meant the ownership vacuum was structural. In organizations, it means the same thing.

Same instinct. Forty people in a bar became an org chart. The diagnostic move is identical.

· · ·

What changed between the bar and the consulting work is what I have to do with what I find.

At O'Toole's, the read resolved into action immediately. The customer was about to become a problem; I called the cab before he did. On the floor, the defect cluster pointed upstream and the documentation captured it. In the ticket queue, the diagnosis pointed to the fix.

In consulting, the finding is not the finish. It's the beginning.

What I find in an organization's AI readiness, the ownership gap, the reality split, the accountability void, has to become something that a person inside the organization can act on after I've left. That's the entire architecture of the engagement. I'm not there to fix what I find. I'm there to find the person who can fix what I find, and make the finding transferable to them.

Post 04 named the gap the piano forced me to see: the instinct reads the pattern, but reading isn't playing. The knowledge doesn't close the distance between where your hands are and where the notes are. The body closes it, on its own schedule, through its own repetition.

In consulting, closing that gap is the product. You find the person inside the organization with enough domain depth that they can already half-read the pattern, they just don't have the framework to act on what they're seeing. Then you give them the framework. And then you leave, because your staying would just mean the gap was never closed; it was only bridged.

It's the only answer I have to the limit the piano surfaced. Not a solution. A workaround built on the right architecture.

· · ·

The series started with one question: is it the same skill across five domains, or did it mutate?

I still don't know.

The processing feels continuous, below naming, faster than decision, finding structure before the vocabulary arrives. The substrate changes. The noise level changes. The distance from the actual signal changes. What runs underneath all of it feels like the same processor. But I can't see it from outside itself, and it has no way to verify its own continuity.

The bar was forty people and a room where I could watch what happened. The floor was objects that held still. The ticket queue was text, which lies by omission. The piano was a pattern I could see and couldn't yet play. The org is people and processes and the gap between what they report and what they do, which is the bar again, at a different scale, with a different kind of accountability attached to getting the read wrong.

There's a line through all of it. I can trace it. Whether it's one line or five very similar ones drawn in the same hand is a question the series can't answer. That's probably the right place to end it.

The instinct doesn't know what it's reading. It just reads.

That might be the only thing I'm certain of.
