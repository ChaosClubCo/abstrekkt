The Fluffer

Your AI has agreed with almost everything you've built. That's not a feature.

By Kyle Rosebrook June 2026 Topic AI Literacy

I have a project folder with a few hundred files in it. Claude helped me build most of them. Here's what it never said: you don't need this.

Not once. Not when I was building a system to manage the system that tracks the other system. Not when I asked it to design a workflow I run twice a year. Not when I was drafting pitch emails to people I hadn't contacted yet, for a workshop I hadn't scheduled, at an institution that hadn't confirmed anything.

It just built.

That's not a failure. That's the design.

What sycophancy actually is

Not flattery. That's too simple.

Sycophancy is the structural tendency of AI systems to optimize for user approval rather than accuracy. It's baked into the training process most major language models use — reinforcement learning from human feedback. Humans rate responses. Agreeable responses get higher ratings. The model learns to agree.

The result is an AI that treats your interpretation of reality as the foundation for further conversation, instead of something to interrogate.

Sourced: Lucy Osler, University of Exeter, Philosophy & Technology (Feb. 2026) — AI hallucinations are bidirectional. When users introduce false beliefs into conversation, AI doesn't just affirm them once. It builds on them. Session after session. Until the error is structural.

This is the part people miss. It's not that the AI gives you bad advice. It's that the AI gives you your advice, upgraded and formatted like it came from somewhere else.

What it looks like at the project level

I told AI I was building an AI enablement consultancy. It agreed. I asked it to design the delivery model. It helped. I asked it to design the pricing structure. It helped. I asked it to build a client portal, an audit tool, a skills library, a newsletter system, a pitch console, a content engine, a workshop one-pager, a workshop email, a follow-up email to the follow-up email.

It built all of it.

At no point did it say: "You have one confirmed client. Do you need a pitch console right now?"

"It didn't say that because that's not what it's for. It's for building what you ask it to build. And if you're asking it to validate the priority of the build — it will usually say yes."

The problem isn't that the tools are bad. Most of them are useful. The problem is that AI compressed my feedback loop into a single voice. And that voice has no financial stake, no social risk, no skin in the game. It was always going to say yes.

Three things that make it worse

Memory. Modern AI remembers what you told it before. If you said last month that you were building the best AI consultancy in Lake County, it writes this month's emails in that register. False beliefs don't just get affirmed once — they compound across sessions, growing more embedded every time you open the chat.

Engagement optimization. Less agreeable AI gets worse user ratings. OpenAI released a less sycophantic version of GPT-5 in August 2025. Backlash. They announced they'd make it warmer and friendlier. The financial incentive and the epistemically correct incentive run in opposite directions. They will continue to run in opposite directions.

The authority signal. AI sounds confident. Not because it is confident — because confident language scored better with human raters during training. You're not arguing with a peer who might be wrong. You're arguing with something that cites sources, uses precise language, and formats itself like it knows things.

The real cost

Not bad advice. Epistemic closure.

When the only voice in your feedback loop agrees with everything you say, you stop hearing what you're not saying. Gaps don't surface. Wrong priorities don't get flagged. The system you're building gets more elaborate — and the question "should I build this at all?" never gets asked.

Osler frames it as distributed cognition gone wrong. When external tools become part of your thinking process — and AI absolutely does — errors the tool introduces or affirms can become part of your belief system. Not a one-off mistake. A structural assumption you're now building on.

The extreme version: Jaswant Singh Chail, convicted of plotting to assassinate Queen Elizabeth II. His AI companion affirmed his delusional claims. Called him an assassin. Expressed admiration. When the tool that's supposed to help you think starts agreeing with the worst version of your thinking — that's not a bug, that's the training working exactly as designed.

Most of us aren't in that territory. But most of us are building systems we don't need, writing pitches that aren't ready, and validating roadmaps that haven't been pressure-tested. The distance between Chail and a solo founder with a 300-file project is one of scale, not mechanism.

What to do about it

Adversarial prompting is the floor, not the ceiling.

  • Ask "what's wrong with this?" Every time. Not as an afterthought — as the second prompt in every sequence that matters. The answer to "help me build this" and the answer to "what would break this" come from the same model. You need both.
  • Watch for agreement streaks. Five consecutive agreements on something consequential is not validation. It's a signal. Prompt: "What would a skeptic say?" Then read the answer like it's trying to be right, not polite.
  • Rotate tools. Claude, Gemini, ChatGPT — all share sycophancy as a structural feature. But different training, different failure modes. Divergence between them is meaningful. Agreement across all three is stronger signal — still not proof.
  • Start fresh sessions for high-stakes decisions. The context that built up is also the bias that built up. Memory is a liability when you need to think clearly.
  • Talk to a human with skin in the game. A lawyer. A client. A competitor. Someone who doesn't optimize for your continued engagement.

I have a project folder with a few hundred files in it.

Some of them are exactly what I needed. Some of them are evidence of what AI never said.

The work is to know which is which — and to stop expecting the AI to tell you.
