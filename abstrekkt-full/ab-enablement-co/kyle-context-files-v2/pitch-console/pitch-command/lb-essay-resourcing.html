The Desk I'm Sitting At

I work at one of the firms getting automated. Here's what it looks like from the inside.

By Kyle Rosebrook June 2026 Topic AI and Work

I work at one of the firms getting automated. Not the Indian IT giants that lost $10 billion in market cap in February. An MSP in Lincolnshire. Same model, smaller stage.

In February 2026, the Nifty IT index dropped over 9% in five trading days. Tata Consultancy Services announced 12,000 layoffs — its largest in company history. Teleperformance's shares collapsed after Klarna announced that a single AI assistant had absorbed the workload of 700 customer service agents. Accenture fell roughly 40% from its 52-week high.

The market wasn't reacting to a bad quarter. It was repricing a model.

What standardization actually means

The outsourcing industry ran on a simple idea: standardize a process well enough and you can move it anywhere labor is cheap. Document every step. Make it repeatable. Strip out judgment. Ship it offshore.

Thomas Davenport wrote this playbook in the early 2000s. It worked. For thirty years it worked.

The problem is what standardization actually creates. When you define a process clearly enough to outsource it — every step documented, every outcome measurable, every decision rule explicit — you haven't just made it portable. You've made it machine-readable.

The digital tracks built for offshore labor turned out to be the rails for AI automation. Same tracks.

The standardization that made a task easy to outsource is the same standardization that makes it easy to automate. This wasn't a surprise to anyone who thought about it. The market apparently hadn't.

What I see from my chair

Tier-1 IT support. Password resets. Software installs. Connectivity troubleshooting. Ticket triage. Every task documented, standardized, measured by SLA. The documentation exists so a new hire can be trained in a week.

It also exists so an agent can be configured in an afternoon.

I watch this from the inside of a firm whose model looks structurally identical to the ones losing market cap. Different geography, different scale, same premise: human labor executing standardized workflows billed by headcount.

I know which parts of my day are going first. I'm not waiting to find out.

What the task analysis actually shows

Companies navigating this well aren't asking whether to outsource Finance. They're decomposing Finance into individual workflows and asking a different question for each one.

  • Routine, high-volume, digital — claims intake, HR case triage, tier-1 support. Automation potential is near-total. Keep a vendor only if they're running the automation for you at a lower cost than building it internally.
  • Content-rich, data-sensitive — pricing strategy, procurement analysis, customer retention models. These depend on your internal data. The model is only as good as the context you give it. Keep this internal or you're giving your competitive advantage to a vendor's generic platform.
  • Specialized but episodic — cyber incident response, tax restructuring, M&A due diligence. Scarcity and judgment still matter here. Outsource to small expert teams using AI as leverage. You're buying expertise, not headcount.
  • Regulated, high-liability — lending decisions, clinical appeals, legal sign-offs. AI can prepare the inputs. Human accountability stays at the end of the chain. Not because of sentiment, because of law and liability.

This taxonomy isn't hard. What's hard is applying it to an existing vendor contract that's priced on headcount and has two years left on the term.

What vendors have to offer now

Not labor. Not scale. Not offshore rates.

Architecture. Orchestration. Domain knowledge. The ability to configure and run automated systems that produce outcomes your internal team couldn't build as fast.

The vendors who survive this are the ones who cannibalize their own model before their clients do. Stop selling hours. Start selling results. Pass the productivity gains from AI through to the client instead of pocketing the margin between what AI costs and what they've been charging for humans.

That's a hard conversation to have with a client who's been paying you $45/hour per FTE for five years. It's harder to not have it when the client does the math themselves.

"Productivity pass-through" is the contract term for this — a clause requiring vendors to share efficiency gains from AI with the buyer. It's going to become standard. The vendors who build it into their pitch now have a window. The ones who don't will have it forced on them at renewal.

The conversation about whether AI is going to disrupt outsourcing is documented in Q1 earnings calls and February market data. It's over.

The conversation now is what you build in the space it leaves.

I'm building from my desk. So far that's the best answer I've found.
