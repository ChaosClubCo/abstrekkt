AUTHOR'S NOTE — FOR GRANT REVIEWERS

This work sample contains two excerpts from a book-length literary memoir currently in active development. The book is structured as a letter to my daughter, Kinsley.

The first excerpt — "Before the Break" — is drawn from the O'Toole's chapter, covering my years bartending in Milwaukee before a traumatic brain injury caused by overdose. This material has been excavated through a structured scene-development process and drafted in fragments that are now being assembled into a continuous chapter.

The second excerpt — "The Card Table" — is from the chapter about building a life coach's website on a folding card table in 2015, which I've identified as the origin of my self-teaching method and, by extension, the entire construction arc the book documents.

Both excerpts are works in progress. Both are in the author's authentic voice. The prose has not been through a full editorial pass. That is what this grant would make possible.

The thesis: I did not recover from the injury, the addiction, or the years they produced. I constructed a replacement system. A reader who finishes this book thinking "brave recovery story" has read the wrong book.

PART ONE: BEFORE THE BREAK

— O'Toole's —


The bar at O'Toole's on a Tuesday afternoon was dim, familiar, forgiving, and the trick was to get there before the lunch crowd thinned out, before the stools emptied and the bartender had nothing to do but watch you. Volume was camouflage. You needed the noise — the sports recap bleeding from two different TVs, the waitress calling out ticket numbers, the guy three seats down laughing too hard at something his buddy said. In a full bar, a man drinking alone at 1pm is background. In a quiet bar, he's a problem. So you timed it. Walked in around 11:45, ordered something with food attached to it so there was a plate involved, a performance of normalcy with ranch dressing. The Jameson came second. Or third. The count was never the point. The point was the particular quality of afternoon light through tinted windows — that amber wash that made everything look like it was already a memory, already soft at the edges, already something that happened to someone else. You could breathe in there. That was the whole thing. You could just breathe.


*


Fran and Stewart needed their Tuesday night pre-dinner — gin martini, extra olive, and whatever was closest to a Shirley Temple without being called one — before they said anything. Before coats were off, before the host stand, before they'd even made eye contact with me across the room. I just knew. Not from training, not from some chart on a break-room wall about reading body language. Something fucking easier than that: they were tired, they came here every Tuesday, and I was paying attention. The golden rule with a service industry translation — if you walked in looking like the day had taken a piece of you, you wanted the thing that always made it better waiting when you sat down. I had it ready. That's the whole trick. Not magic. Not intuition. Just the willingness to actually look at the person in front of you and ask yourself what they need before they have to ask.


*


Calm, collected, a little smirk here and there — that was the surface. Underneath it, my jaw was working like I was chewing gravel, arms loose but never still, the kind of stillness that isn't stillness at all but controlled motion waiting for somewhere to go. There's a specific choreography to a team that's running hot — not smooth, not clean, but functional the way a busted engine is functional: loud, burning something it shouldn't, still getting you where you need to go. I was fluent in that language. The smirk wasn't detachment. It was translation. What I knew how to do was read the frequency everyone else was broadcasting and tune myself to it without ever letting on that I was receiving anything at all.


*


On good nights I could hold the orders, the faces, the rotations in front, and still have the go juice running underneath — that warm static behind everything, the hum that kept the signal clear. Two processors, both online. The go juice didn't slow me down. That's the lie I told and the thing that was also, for a while, just true. On bad nights the second processor crashed first. The hum went choppy, then silent, and what was left was just the raw feed — and nothing underneath to buffer it. I'd find someone to blame before I even registered I was doing it, voice already raised, the words already out and uglier than they needed to be. The dual-track wasn't a skill. It was a dependency dressed up as competence, and when one rail went down, I took everyone near me with it.


*


Fran and Stewart asked the bar if I'd work their son's wedding. Not just asked if someone was available — asked for me by name. Patrick came and found me during a Tuesday close, told me like he was doing me a favor, which he was. Something in my chest did a thing I wasn't expecting: a small clean warmth, the kind that doesn't announce itself. The manager said no. Weekend availability — Kinsley.

That was the part that never got explained to them. I didn't get to tell Fran that the reason I couldn't come was that somewhere in the middle of the years between then and now, a person existed who I would leave work at 1pm every Friday to pick up from school. That the custody schedule that blocked me from their son's wedding was the clearest evidence I had that something had been constructed correctly, even while other things were still failing.

The bar didn't know about Kinsley. The bar only knew the version of me that showed up on time, ran the rail clean, and kept the jaw loose enough that nobody asked if I was all right.

I was not always all right.

The diagnostic instinct was.


*


The age comes out different every time I tell it. Nineteen, I say, and then someone corrects me or I correct myself and it becomes twenty-one, and then I'm not sure which one I'm arguing for or why. Somewhere in there. That's as precise as it gets.

I know this: O'Toole's was mine for four years. I knew every face before they ordered. Not because I had a good memory — the imaging would confirm later, years later, that I don't — but because I had a different thing. Pattern recognition that didn't require storage. The machine kept running when the filing system started failing.

That's what I didn't know I was working with. The book is about what I did when I couldn't trust it to run on its own anymore.

PART TWO: THE CONSTRUCTION

— The Card Table —


The table is a card table.

Not a desk. Not a standing workstation with cable management and a monitor arm bolted to something structural. A card table — the kind that folds in half and leans against a wall when company comes, except company doesn't come much and it hasn't folded since I set it up. On it right now: two monitors, an RGB keyboard that I did not need and bought anyway because the purple and pink glow does something for the brain at 1:30am that I don't fully understand and have stopped trying to justify, a Toshiba remote that belongs to a TV I can't see from here, a yellow highlighter, a green drink, cables going places I've stopped tracking. Stack of papers on the right side. Notebooks underneath those. A pen that works and one that doesn't and I keep reaching for both.

This is the command center. This is where everything gets built.

I built Bear's website here.

Bear is Soaring Bear — Sacred Spiritual Healing Journeys, Deep Soul Mentorship, permitted guide in Sedona, the man my mother sent me to when she was out of options and I was out of arguments. I went because I had nothing left to try. The commitment I made going in wasn't I'm going to work harder than I've ever worked. It was closer to: I've tried at the bottom and I'm still down in this fucking hole. I was wrong about those being the answer, let's try the opposite because I seriously have nothing fucking left. That's not determination. That's a person who has run out of road and turned around and tried the other direction because standing still wasn't working.

Bear received me when I was still mostly wreckage. Way later, I built his website.

I didn't know what I was doing. No Figma, no vocabulary for what the things were called, no framework for how the pieces connected. I was learning the way you learn when the credential path is closed — Loyola didn't accept me, CLC couldn't hold me, and I'd walked into the College of Lake County University Center looking for a door that turned out not to be open for someone with my transcript. So I sat down at a folding table and started clicking through things until they made sense, and then I took a screenshot so I'd remember where I'd been, and then I clicked through more things.

The screenshot wasn't a technique. It was the first prosthetic. My brain couldn't hold the state of what I was doing, so I made the screen remember instead.

That's the whole origin story. A person who couldn't trust their memory taking pictures of their screen.

It started with an SVG image generator — print-on-demand, trying to get rich quick when AI started breaking open. I didn't get rich. The tool stayed anyway. Wrong reason, right direction. That's been the pattern the whole time: I show up with the wrong motive and end up somewhere useful. One tool led to another. Each one solving a problem the previous one had created or revealed. Each one marking a moment when I discovered that an organic function was no longer reliable and needed something outside myself to replace it.

The card table is still here. The same folding legs, the same surface I built Bear's website on. Bear sent his coaching assignments through a ticket system I built on the same table. The construction started here and never stopped.

The bar in Milwaukee had amber afternoon light and I could breathe in there. The card table at 1:30am has an RGB keyboard and I can build on here. The mechanism is the same. The medium changed. The relief is different — this one makes something instead of obscuring something.

That's the construction. That's what the book is.
