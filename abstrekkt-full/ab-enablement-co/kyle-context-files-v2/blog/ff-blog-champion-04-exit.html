The Exit: Why Firing Yourself Is the Product

Most consultants are rewarded for your dependency. The champion model is rewarded for your independence. That structural difference is why it works.

Oct 2026 AI Enablement 4 min read

Most consultants tell you what they're worth. I tell you when I'm done. Done means your champion can build workflows I didn't design, answer questions I wasn't asked, and teach a colleague what I taught them. Done means I'm no longer needed. That's the product.

This is a strange argument to make when trying to sell an engagement. It's worth making anyway, because the strangeness is the point.

The Dependency Trap

Most consulting is structured around time — per hour, per month, per project — which means the consultant's income scales with your continued need for them. That's not a conspiracy. It's just how the incentive structure works. The consultant who helps you become self-sufficient faster is earning less per relationship. The consultant who introduces new problems as fast as they solve old ones is earning more.

That incentive exists whether anyone acknowledges it or not. It shapes decisions about what to hand off and what to keep, about what to document and what to keep in someone's head, about when an engagement is "ready to close" versus "needs another month."

The dependency model
Income scales with your ongoing need for the consultant
Undocumented knowledge keeps the relationship alive
New problems extend the contract
Your independence reduces the consultant's revenue
The exit model
Income comes from clean exits that produce case studies
Documented knowledge is the deliverable, not the retained asset
Your independence is the proof the model works
Fast exits free capacity for the next engagement

The champion model is built on the second column. I grow by completing engagements quickly and well, not by extending them indefinitely. That means my incentive is the same as yours: get your champion to self-sufficiency as fast as possible.

I'm not rewarded for your dependency. That's not a sales pitch. It's why the model is worth trusting before you've seen it work.

What "Done" Looks Like in Practice

Most consulting relationships close on a date. Ninety days. Six months. End of the contract term. The champion model closes on outcomes — three specific signals that the champion has reached genuine self-sufficiency, regardless of how much time has passed.

1
They adapt without calling
The champion encounters a situation that wasn't in the training — a new client type, an unexpected output, a workflow that evolved. They work through it. They figure out what the AI is doing and why. They adjust. The first time this happens without a call to me, the engagement has produced something real.
2
They teach a colleague without being asked
Not "here's the workflow I was taught" — they explain the underlying logic. Why the workflow is designed the way it is. What to do when the output is unexpected. They're not passing forward a procedure; they're passing forward understanding. That's the moment the knowledge becomes your organization's, not mine.
3
They call with solutions, not questions
The character of the check-ins shifts. Early in an engagement, the calls are: "What do I do here?" At the end, they're: "I tried this. Here's what happened. Does this track?" That shift — from asking for answers to asking for confirmation — is the ownership signal. The champion is no longer learning from me. They're thinking alongside me. That's the exit.

All three can happen inside thirty days with the right candidate. They can take longer with a harder starting point. The calendar isn't the measure. The outcomes are.

What Comes After the Exit

Once the engagement closes, the champion has internal capability. They don't need me to maintain it.

That's a different relationship than most consulting produces. Most engagements end with the client dependent on the consultant's knowledge — which means keeping access to that knowledge requires a monthly payment. The retainer isn't optional; it's structural. The consultant built it that way, even if nobody said so out loud.

The champion model produces the opposite: a client who can call when they want to extend their capability, not when they need to maintain it. Some do. Some don't. A referral from a client who doesn't need ongoing support is more valuable than a retainer from a client who does — because it proves the model, not just the relationship.

The outcomes I'm working toward at the end of every engagement, in order: a champion who owns their AI capability, a case study I can use with the next client, and a referral to someone who has the same problem. The retainer is fourth. It only makes sense if the champion has grown past what we built together and wants to build further.

The consultant who makes themselves unnecessary fastest is the one worth hiring. If that argument sounds strange, it's worth asking why.
