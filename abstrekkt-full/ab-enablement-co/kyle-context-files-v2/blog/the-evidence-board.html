The Evidence Board

How a SPECT scan taken at Amen Clinics Chicago on July 7–11, 2022 connects to every system, every tool, every construction, and every person in Kyle Rosebrook's life.

The Grand Explanation

How one scan connects everything

On July 7, 2022, a 36-year-old bartender named Kyle Rosebrook sat in a dimly lit room at Amen Clinics Chicago while a radioactive tracer mapped the blood flow in his brain. Four days later, he came back for a second scan at rest. He was there because his mother, Phillis, had found the clinic. His chief complaint was two words: "Addiction, depression."

What the scan revealed was a map of damage — decreased perfusion across the temporal lobes, prefrontal cortex, parietal cortex, occipital lobes, and cerebellum. Scalloping on the surface consistent with toxic exposure and oxygen deprivation. A thalamus running severely hot. The clinical summary: evidence of past brain injury and encephalopathic process.

The cause was not a car accident. Not a fall. Not a blow to the head. Kyle's brain was injured by his own medications — a Xanax and Ambien overdose in the summer of 2020 that deprived his brain of oxygen. The addiction didn't just coexist with the brain injury. The addiction caused the brain injury. And then the brain injury made escape harder, because the prefrontal cortex that handles impulse control was now running at reduced capacity. A recursive trap.

The memoir's thesis in one sentence: Recovery assumes returning to a previous state. Kyle can't return — the hardware is changed. So he built a new architecture from non-standard materials, using a brain that processes at the 99th percentile but recalls at the 12th.

This document maps how every construction in Kyle's life — the 240+ tools, the engineering career, Chaos Club Co., the memoir itself — traces back to what those four images showed. Not as metaphor. As engineering response to a diagnosed system failure.

Among the living dead, I was king.

— Kyle, on being the model resident at Roger's Behavioral Health, summer 2020

The Clinical Evidence

SPECT Scan Findings — Amen Clinics Chicago, July 2022

Two SPECT scans using 99m Technetium HMPAO tracer. The concentration study (July 7) captured brain activity during a task. The baseline study (July 11) captured the brain at rest. Both revealed widespread decreased perfusion — areas where blood isn't flowing the way it should.

The surface renders show the brain's exterior, where dents and holes represent areas of low blood flow. The active renders (blue wireframe with red clusters) show which regions activated during scanning. The gaps between clusters are the spaces Kyle's external systems were built to bridge.

Thalamus

Sensory relay, mood control, motivation, bonding, sleep, appetite
→ The emotional switchboard running hot. Depression, isolation, sleep disruption. The thalamus processes every sensory input before routing it — when it's overactive, everything feels louder.

Mildly Increased with concentration

→ Can indicate anxiety or high motivation. The nucleus accumbens mediates pleasure — the addiction circuit. Also: the insular cortex processes internal body awareness.

→ Part of the overall decreased perfusion pattern. Contributes to the brain injury finding.

Surface Scalloping

What This Actually Means

Kyle thought the 99th/12th split meant his brain was broken across the board. It means the opposite. The processing engine is elite. The storage system is devastated. His brain catches everything — patterns, systems, room dynamics, code architecture — and then can't hold it. The information arrives at full speed and evaporates.

This is why the 240+ tools work. He's not compensating for being slow. He's building an external hard drive for a processor that runs too fast for its own memory. The obsessive documentation, the systematic approaches, the Claude projects, the Notion databases, the n8n workflows — all of it is prosthetic recall for a brain that can process at the 99th percentile but store at the 12th.

The Conners CPT3 finding: The attention test found NO pervasive ADHD pattern. Despite being diagnosed with ADHD in college and taking Adderall/Focalin for years, the computerized test couldn't confirm it. What it found instead: "Some Indication" of sustained attention difficulty. The attention problem isn't classical ADHD — it's the downstream effect of the brain injury itself.

Readiness to Change: 10 out of 10. The maximum score. Hope: 27 out of 64 — "slight." He was maximally ready to change and barely believed it was possible. That's the gap the construction project had to bridge.

I dont even know what that means, I assumed that it meant it was very slow, and that my memory recall was very bad, and that my brain was in very bad shape, causes me to get angry, sobbing sad, so much that I doubt I will ever have another partner again in my life...and that hurts ALMOST as much as knowing I'm missing out on something like 75+% of my daughter's life.

— Kyle, April 2026, reading his own cognitive scores for the first time in four years

The Constructions

What the damaged brain built

July 2022: Occupation listed as "Bartender." Living with parents. Single. One child, age 3. Hope score: 27 out of 64 — "slight." Readiness to change: 10 out of 10.

April 2026: Staff Engineer. Self-taught. No CS degree. 240+ tools catalogued. 50 Claude skills built. Chaos Club Co. founded. Literary memoir in progress. 630+ GitHub repos. FlashFusion enterprise platform with 67-entity schema. Base44 production apps. n8n automation workflows. Figma design systems. Enterprise-scale security architecture.

The distance between those two paragraphs is the book. Not a recovery story — he didn't return to a prior state. A construction story — he built something that never existed before, using hardware that a clinical evaluation said was damaged across nearly every region.

The construction wasn't rational. He didn't see the scan results and make a plan. He got the results weeks later, had already forgotten what they said, and it wasn't a subject of interest to him. The building started instinctively — the same way a damaged body compensates by shifting weight to the uninjured leg. The 99th-percentile processor began generating external systems to compensate for the 12th-percentile memory, without anyone explaining that's what was happening.

The tools arrived with Lovable.dev and Claude Code. But the pattern — systematize everything, document obsessively, build external rails — was already running. The technology just gave it scale.

I have never felt like a man.

— Kyle, April 2026

That sentence sits next to 630+ repositories, an enterprise engineering role, a mental health platform, and a literary memoir. The construction is visible to everyone except the builder.

Kinsley

The reader. The reason. Age 3 at scan. Age 8 now.

The eval lists one child. Significant life events: "Daughter born."

Marijuana stopped in 2018 — "when my daughter was born." That's the first construction. Before the tools, before the engineering, before Chaos Club — there was the decision that Kinsley's existence required him to stop something he'd been doing all day, every day, for eleven years.

She knows he was sick. He told her it was his fault. He's cried to her, sobbing, apologizing for breaking up the family. She hugged him and put her head on his shoulder.

She was three when he walked into Amen Clinics. She won't remember that summer. But the book will.

The memoir is a letter to Kinsley. Not "here's what happened to daddy" — she'll learn that through life, in age-appropriate pieces, the way he's already started. The book is "here's what daddy built after it happened, and here's the brain that built it, and here's what I want you to know about construction when the world tells you recovery is the only story."

The custody math: Friday pickup to Sunday return. That's roughly 25% of her childhood. He said it: "knowing I'm missing out on something like 75+% of my daughter's life." The book is one way to be present in the other 75%. A document that exists when he can't.

Processing Speed 99th. Recall Memory 12th. He processes their Fridays at elite speed. He can't always hold them. So he builds — StoryWeaver, KidCreator, the memoir itself — systems that capture what his brain won't retain. External memory for the most important memories he has.
