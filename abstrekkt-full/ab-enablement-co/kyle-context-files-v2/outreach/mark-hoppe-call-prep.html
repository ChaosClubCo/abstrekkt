Call Objective

What success looks like

Win Condition

Mark ends the call with a specific next step in mind — either a follow-up call with more people in the room, a look at something you've built, or an intro to someone else inside Central States. He remembers you as a practitioner who gets it, not another vendor talking about AI potential.

What you need to learn

  • Where are they in their AI maturity? (Experimenting, scaling, or stuck?)
  • What's his actual scope — applications only, or does he touch infrastructure, ops, workflow?
  • What are the real friction points — adoption, governance, tooling, budget, executive buy-in?
  • Who else is involved in AI decisions? Does he own this or influence it?
  • Is there a vendor relationship already, or are they figuring this out internally?
  • What would make his life materially easier in the next 90 days?

What you want him to remember about you

Your Impression
  • You're someone who has actually built AI workflows, not just studied them.
  • You work inside a real environment — multiple clients, real constraints, real failures and fixes.
  • You ask good questions and actually listen to the answers.
  • You're not trying to sell him anything today. You're worth keeping in the loop.

Pre-Call Mental Framing

How to think before you dial

The Frame

You are not a vendor. You are not pitching. You are a practitioner having a peer conversation with someone who's working the same problem from a different seat. He's already using AI — that means he has opinions, frustrations, and questions. Your job is to surface those and be useful to them.

You have real standing here. You're the documented AI SME at an MSP. That means you've seen AI implementation across multiple business environments — not just your own. That breadth is rare and valuable. You're not overselling when you mention it. You're being accurate.

The 3 mental anchors

Curious, not impressive Listen first, talk second One next step, not a deal

If you feel yourself starting to ramp up and over-explain — stop. Ask a question instead. The goal of this call is not to tell him everything. It's to earn the next conversation.

What keeps you calm

  • He already opted in. John set this up. Mark texted first. The room is warm.
  • He's driving. This is casual. It's not a boardroom pitch.
  • You don't need to land a contract today. You need to be memorable for the right reasons.
  • If you blank on something, curiosity saves you. Ask him something.

What to avoid mentally

Treating this like a performance. You're not auditioning. You're connecting.
Front-loading everything you know. Save it for when he asks.
Worrying about the close. The close is just asking what happens next.

Live Call Flow

1
Opening — Acknowledge & Orient
Objective

Land warm. Confirm he's comfortable. Set a casual tone. Don't over-formalize a call that started as a text from a mutual friend.

Listen for

His energy level, background noise (he's driving), how much time he signals, whether he immediately redirects to business or stays casual.

Don't open with your resume.
Lead with gratitude and curiosity about him.
2
Rapport — Let Him Land First
Objective

Get him talking about himself before you say much about you. Directors of Applications have a lot going on. Let him feel heard in the first 60 seconds.

Listen for

Anything about his current workload, recent AI wins or headaches, team size or scope signals, and how he frames his role.

3
Positioning — Who You Are, One Clean Pass
Objective

Give him exactly enough to place you. Self-taught AI SME, MSP environment, practical operator — not a vendor, not a consultant pitching. Keep it to 3–4 sentences max, then ask something.

Don't list tools, certs, or projects unprompted.
Say one specific, credible thing and let it sit.
4
Discovery — Find the Real Problem
Objective

Ask 2–3 open questions about where they are with AI. Not "are you using AI" — he is. Ask about friction, maturity, what's working, what isn't.

Listen for

Adoption friction (people not using tools), governance gaps, automation requests backing up, one specific thing that's causing pain, who else is involved in decisions.

5
Value Alignment — Match Without Pitching
Objective

Reflect what he said back to him in a way that shows you understand it at a deeper level. Don't pitch — just demonstrate pattern recognition. "Yeah, that's actually a really common place for things to stall..."

6
Soft Credibility — One Specific Story
Objective

Drop one concrete thing you've built or solved that maps to something he mentioned. Not a list. One example, told in 30 seconds. Let it land before moving on.

7
Transition to Opportunity
Objective

Signal that there might be something worth exploring further — without pressure. "I don't want to keep you too long, but I'd love to follow up on some of this when you're not behind the wheel."

8
Close — One Clear Next Step
Objective

Agree on a single next action. Send a follow-up text, schedule a proper call, or get an intro to someone specific. Don't leave it at "we should talk more sometime."

Full Call Script

Opening

"Hey Mark, it's Kyle. Thanks for texting — and thanks for being open to this. I know you're on the road so I'll keep it easy. I appreciate John making the connection."

Then pause. Let him respond. He may take it somewhere or signal how much time he has.

If he asks "how do you know John?"

"John and my mom go way back — and I used to bartend at O'Toole's years ago, so we've crossed paths a few times over the years. Good guy."

Rapport — Get Him Talking

"So Director of Applications at Central States — that's a big seat with a lot of moving pieces. I'm curious, are you more on the systems and integrations side, or does your scope stretch into the workflow and process layer too?"

This is not small talk. It tells you how wide his influence is. Let him answer fully.

Positioning — Who You Are

"For context on my end — I work in technology at an MSP, and I've sort of become the AI person there. Self-taught, no formal AI background — I just started building things, figured out what actually worked in real environments, and it kind of became my lane. I've been heads-down in practical implementation for a while now — less about the hype, more about what actually sticks when you push it into a real workflow."
Don't say "I'm an AI expert" or "I have 240+ tools." Let the work speak through specifics, not scale.

Discovery — Open Questions

"Since you mentioned you're already using AI — where are you in that journey at Central States? Like, are you in the early-adopter phase, or are you past that and dealing more with the scaling and governance questions?"

This gives him room to go wherever he actually is, not where you assume.

"What's the version of AI that's actually landing with your team right now — like, what are people actually using versus what's kind of sitting there unused?"

Adoption friction is where most orgs are stuck. This question surfaces it without assuming it.

"What's the thing that's been hardest to solve? Like, where does AI keep promising something and then not quite delivering in practice?"

This earns trust fast. You're not there to tell him AI is magic. You're there because you know it isn't always.

Value Alignment — Pattern Recognition Response

After he describes a pain point, reflect it back with depth:

"Yeah — that's actually a really common stall point. A lot of orgs get through the pilot phase and then hit this wall where the tool works but adoption doesn't. Usually it's because the workflow change wasn't designed around how people actually work, just around what the tool can do."
"That governance piece is real. I've seen it slow things down significantly — not because people don't want to use AI, but because nobody's defined what 'appropriate use' actually looks like in context."
"The integration layer is usually where things get interesting. The models aren't the hard part — it's the plumbing. Connecting AI into actual systems of record without creating new fragility."

Use whichever resonates with what he actually said. Don't use all three.

Soft Credibility — One Concrete Example

Drop one thing you've actually built that maps to his pain. Pick from below based on what surfaced in discovery:

If he mentioned adoption friction
"I ran into the exact same thing. I ended up building out a skills library — basically structured AI workflows that non-technical people could actually use without having to prompt-engineer anything. Brought the friction way down. That's the kind of thing I think about a lot."
If he mentioned process or workflow automation
"I've built out a decent amount of multi-step AI workflow automation — connecting models into real business processes, not just standalone chat. The interesting part is always the edge cases, where the automation breaks and you need a human back in the loop intelligently."
If he mentioned governance or compliance
"Governance is actually where my AppSec background comes in. I think about AI through a security lens pretty naturally — what's leaving the org, what's being generated, who can see what. That constraint actually makes for better implementations, not worse ones."

Transition to Opportunity

"I don't want to take up your whole drive — but this has been a really good conversation. I feel like there's actual overlap here worth exploring properly when you're not behind the wheel. Would it be worth getting something on the calendar, even just a 30-minute follow-up?"

If he says yes

"Perfect. I'll send you a text after this — you can just reply with a couple times that work and we'll lock something in."

Closing the call

"Really appreciate you taking the time, Mark. Safe drive — and I'll be in touch."

Listening Support Layer

Anchor reminders — use these when you feel yourself drifting

What did he just say? Stay with his words Don't plan your next line He knows more about his problem than you do Silence is okay

What his words might actually mean

If he says "we're already doing a lot with AI"

Translation: He's proud of it. Don't challenge. Ask what's working best. There's almost always a "but" coming.

If he says "we have a lot of initiatives going on"

Translation: Time is limited, priorities are competing, and AI may not be top of the stack right now. Ask where AI fits in the priority order.

If he says "we're still figuring out governance"

Translation: This is a real, active problem. This is your opening. Engage here.

If he says "we're working with [vendor]"

Translation: There's already a relationship. Don't compete with it. Ask how that's going and look for the gap.

If he says "interesting, tell me more about what you do"

Translation: He's genuinely curious. This is the moment to give your 60-second positioning — clean and confident, not exhaustive.

How to buy time without sounding awkward

"That's a good point — let me think about that for a second."
"Yeah — say more about that. What does that look like in practice for you?"
"Interesting. Is that a recent thing or has it been building for a while?"

How to respond without breaking flow

Backchanneling — while he's talking, brief acknowledgments keep the energy going: "yeah," "right," "mmhmm," "that makes sense." Don't fill silence with words.

Reflect before responding — after he finishes, repeat back the core of what he said in one sentence before you respond. "So it sounds like the tooling is there but the team isn't quite using it consistently yet — is that right?" This buys you a beat and shows you were listening.

Anticipation Prep

Questions he's likely to ask

"So what exactly do you do?"
"I work in technology at an MSP — managed services, so we're supporting multiple business environments at once. That's where the AI angle came from. I kept running into the same problems across different clients and started building practical solutions. It stuck, and it's become my main focus."
"Are you looking for a job?"
"Not actively — I'm more interested in interesting problems and the people solving them. I saw the AI space and wanted to be in conversations with people who are actually working through it at scale. That's why I appreciated John making the connection."
"What kind of AI work have you actually done?"
"Mostly implementation — building workflows that connect AI into real processes. Some governance and adoption work, some AppSec thinking around AI use. I've built a lot of proof-of-concept work that ended up going into production. Less theoretical, more 'does this actually work in a real environment.'"
"What do you know about our industry?"

If this comes up, be honest about what you don't know and curious about what he does. Don't bluff.

"I know enough to be dangerous — but honestly, you're going to know more about the specific constraints in your world than I do. That's part of why I wanted to hear how you're approaching it."

Likely objections / skepticism

"We're already covered on the AI front."

Don't push. Express genuine interest.

"That's great — honestly, I'm more curious than I am trying to create work. What's working well? Where did you end up landing?"
"We have a vendor for that."
"Totally makes sense. How's that relationship going? Sometimes the vendor has the platform and the org still needs someone who knows how to actually operationalize it internally."
"I'm not sure what you're actually offering."
"Honestly, I'm not either yet — I came into this conversation just wanting to understand what you're working with. If there's a fit somewhere, we'll both feel it. If not, at least we've had a good conversation."

Signals to read

Interested
  • Asks follow-up questions
  • Names specific pain points
  • Says "that's interesting"
  • Mentions other people you should talk to
  • Extends the conversation
Brushing Off
  • Short answers
  • Redirects back to you quickly
  • "We're good on that front"
  • No reciprocal questions
  • Signals he needs to go
Hidden Opportunities
  • If he mentions a specific tool struggle → you may know a fix
  • If he mentions team resistance → adoption frameworks are your lane
  • If he mentions a project stalling → offer to think through it off the call
  • If he mentions someone else on his team → ask if it's worth looping them in

Positioning Language

Self-taught AI SME — how to say it naturally

"I'm self-taught — no formal AI background — but I've been building real implementations for a couple years now and I'm the documented AI SME at my company."
"My background is practical, not academic. I learn by building things and figuring out what breaks."
"I got into AI the same way I got into technology generally — by solving a problem I had and not stopping until I understood how it actually worked."

MSP environment as a credibility amplifier

Use this when it adds breadth, not as your main credential.

"Working in an MSP environment means I've seen AI implementations across a pretty wide range of business contexts — not just one industry or one stack. That cross-exposure is useful when you're trying to figure out what's actually transferable versus what only works in one narrow context."
"The thing about working across multiple clients is you see the same problems surfacing in different forms. It gives you pattern recognition that's hard to get if you're only inside one org."

Practical operator framing

"I'm less interested in what AI can theoretically do and more interested in what actually works when you push it into a real workflow with real people."
"Most of what I build is boring in the best way — it just works, people use it, and it doesn't require anyone to become an AI expert to get value from it."
"I think about adoption as part of the design, not something you figure out after the build."

Business outcomes framing

"The interesting problems aren't usually technical. They're about getting AI to fit how people actually work, not asking people to change how they work to fit AI."
"I've learned more from failed implementations than successful ones — which ones hit the wall and why is usually more instructive than the wins."

Recovery Tools

If you blank

"Give me a second — I want to make sure I answer that properly."
"Actually, I want to flip that back to you for a second — what's your read on it?"
"That's a good question. The honest answer is I'd want to think about it more before I give you a half-baked take."

If you talked too much

"Sorry — I'm going on. What's your experience been with that?"
"I'll stop there. I'm curious what you think."

If he dominates the conversation

This is actually good. Let him talk. When there's a natural pause:

"That's really helpful context. Can I ask — when you say [X], what does that look like day-to-day for your team?"

If the energy drops

"Hey — I don't want to take more of your drive time than makes sense. Is there a better time to continue this when you're not behind the wheel?"
"Let me ask you the most important thing I want to know — and then we can wrap up."

If you missed something important

"Can I go back to something you said a few minutes ago? I want to make sure I understand it right."
"Actually — I glossed over something. When you mentioned [X], what did you mean by that?"

Closing Options

1 — Soft Relationship Close
"This has been a great conversation. I'm not trying to rush anything — I just want to stay on your radar and keep talking. Can I follow up with a text this week?"

Use when: energy is warm but no specific opportunity surfaced. Keeps the door open without pressure.

2 — Exploratory Next Step Close
"It sounds like there might be some actual overlap here worth exploring — especially around [the specific thing he mentioned]. Would you be open to a proper 30-minute call when you're not driving, so I can show you something specific?"

Use when: a specific pain or interest surfaced. Tie the next step to something concrete.

3 — Value-Add Close
"Based on what you described with [X], I actually have something I built that might be directly relevant. Let me send it over — not as a pitch, just as context. If it's useful, great. If not, no harm done."

Use when: a specific use case matched something you've actually built. Only use if the match is real.

4 — Direct Follow-Up Close
"I'll send you a text after this with a couple of calendar links. Even 20 minutes on a proper call would let me give you something more useful than a conversation while you're driving."

Use when: he's clearly interested and you want to lock in something concrete before the call ends.

5 — Long-Game Close
"I'm not going anywhere. Keep me in mind — and if something comes up where having someone who's deep in practical AI implementation would be useful, reach out. I'm genuinely interested in what you're building."

Use when: no immediate fit, but he's someone worth staying in front of. Plants a long-term seed.
