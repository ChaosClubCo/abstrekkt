## Purpose

This document contains the copy, structure, and learning sequence for onboarding people who are AI-curious but do not yet know where they fit. It is built to deploy into any platform (SparkAcademy, SharePoint, Notion, intranet, LMS) with minimal editing.

**Target user:** Someone who says "I want to know more about AI so I can see where I might learn and grow." Not a developer. Not a skeptic. A willing non-expert who needs a first win, not a course.

**What this is NOT:** A comprehensive AI literacy curriculum. That is a later conversation. This is the entry ramp — what moves someone from zero to a genuine first use within one week.

**Where it lives:** SparkAcademy is Abstrekkt's AI literacy platform. This content is the structured layer behind the intake triage widget; the widget routes, this gives users somewhere to land.

## Module 1: What AI Actually Is

> **Headline:** The honest two-minute explanation

### What it is

[TOOL NAME] is a large language model. In plain terms: software trained on an enormous body of text — books, sites, documents — that has learned the patterns in how people write and communicate.

When you type a message, it predicts what a useful, coherent response looks like based on those patterns.

### The analogy that holds

A very well-read colleague who has read nearly everything — but has not done your job. They help you think, write, and research faster. They cannot make the judgment calls that require your context and experience.

### What that means in practice

| AI is good at | AI still needs you for |
|---|---|
| Drafting text you then refine | Final judgment on anything consequential |
| Summarizing long documents | Knowing which information actually matters |
| Generating options to choose from | Choosing |
| Explaining concepts plainly | Verifying claims in high-stakes situations |
| Handling repetitive writing patterns | Relationships, nuance, anything with history |

### The one thing to remember

It will sometimes be wrong with confidence. Not maliciously — it does not know what it does not know. For anything important, verify before you act. For drafting, ideating, and summarizing, the risk is far lower.

## Module 2: What It's Good At

> **Headline:** The four categories where AI earns its keep

Most people who stick with AI use it one of four ways. Find yours.

### 1. The Draft Starter

You know what you need to say. You hate the blank page.

AI gives you a draft to react to instead of a blank page to fill. Your job becomes editing, not writing. This alone saves most people 20–40 minutes a day on written communication.

**This is you if:** You have emails sitting in drafts for days. You write the same kind of message repeatedly with small variations.

### 2. The Summarizer

You have too much to read and not enough time.

AI reads a 40-page document and tells you what matters in 30 seconds. It will not replace reading things that demand careful attention — it eliminates the time spent reading things that turn out not to.

**This is you if:** You read documents before deciding whether they are important. You take meeting notes and never clean them up.

### 3. The Thinking Partner

You have enough information. You need a way to work through it.

AI helps structure a decision, stress-test a plan, or surface angles you are missing. Not because it knows your situation better — because externalizing your thinking and having something push back is a proven way to think more clearly.

**This is you if:** You think out loud. You talk through decisions before making them. You have wished for someone to sanity-check something quickly.

### 4. The Template Machine

You do the same kind of task repeatedly, slightly different each time.

AI builds a reusable template, then fills it faster every time. The first use is the investment. After that, pure return.

**This is you if:** You have thought "I need a better system for this." You reformat or rewrite things you have done before.

## Module 3: Your First Real Prompt

> **Headline:** Run this before you read anything else

### The rule

**Use real work, not a fake example.**

The biggest first-timer mistake is testing AI with a hypothetical — a made-up email to a made-up customer. It produces a generic result, you conclude it is not useful, you move on.

Use something you actually have to do today. The AI has more to work with, the output is genuinely useful, and you see the value immediately.

### The formula

Every effective prompt has three parts:

```
[What you need] + [Key context] + [What good looks like]
```

**Weak:**
> "Write me an email"

**Strong:**
> "Write a follow-up email to a client after a project kickoff call. We aligned on scope, the timeline is 6 weeks, and the next step is them sending access credentials. Professional but not stiff — we have good rapport."

Same request. Entirely different output. The second works because you supplied context.

### Run it now

Open [TOOL NAME]. Paste one of these. Replace the brackets with real information from your work:

**A — Draft something:**
> "I need to write [type of message] to [audience]. The main thing I need them to understand is [key point]. The outcome I want is [response/action]. Write a draft I can edit."

**B — Summarize something:**
> "Here is [document/email/notes]. Summarize it in 3–5 bullet points. Flag anything that requires a decision or action."

**C — Think through something:**
> "I am deciding between [option A] and [option B] for [context]. What are the most important factors? What am I probably not thinking about?"

## Module 5: Common Mistakes (Read Before You Give Up)

> **Headline:** The five things that trip people up in week one

### 1. Being too vague

"Write something about our product" → generic.
"Write a 3-sentence value prop for our invoicing product targeting small business owners who hate accounting software" → usable.

More specific context, better result. If the first output is off, add context and run again.

### 2. Expecting perfection on the first try

AI output is a first draft, not a finished product. The value is not "it wrote the thing." The value is "it gave me something to react to instead of a blank page." Plan to edit.

### 3. Testing with fake tasks

Covered in Module 3. Use real work. Hypotheticals produce generic outputs that hide the real value.

### 4. Treating it like a search engine

AI is not Google. You do not type keywords — you have a conversation. Write full sentences. Give context. If it misses, follow up: "That is not quite what I meant — what I am actually trying to do is [X]."

### 5. Giving up after one bad result

Every first-time user gets at least one bad result. That is the process, not failure. The question is not "did this work?" It is "what would have made this prompt better?" Add that and run again.
