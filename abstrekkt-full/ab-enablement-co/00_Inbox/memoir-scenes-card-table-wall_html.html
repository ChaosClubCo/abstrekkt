Kyle Rosebrook · Mundelein, Illinois · First draft
Scene I — The Card Table

The table is a card table.

Not a desk. Not a standing workstation with cable management and a monitor arm bolted to something structural. A card table — the kind that folds in half and leans against a wall when company comes, except company doesn't come much and it hasn't folded since I set it up. On it right now: two monitors, an RGB keyboard that I did not need and bought anyway because the purple and pink glow does something for the brain at 1:30am that I don't fully understand and have stopped trying to justify, a Toshiba remote that belongs to a TV I can't see from here, a yellow highlighter, a green drink, cables going places I've stopped tracking. Stack of papers on the right side. Notebooks underneath those. A pen that works and one that doesn't and I keep reaching for both.

This is the command center. This is where everything gets built.

I built Bear's website here.

Bear is Soaring Bear — Sacred Spiritual Healing Journeys, Deep Soul Mentorship, permitted guide in Sedona, the man my mother sent me to when she was out of options and I was out of arguments. I went because I had nothing left to try. The commitment I made going in wasn't I'm going to work harder than I've ever worked. It was closer to: I've tried at the bottom and I'm still down in this fucking hole. I was wrong about those being the answer, let's try the opposite because I seriously have nothing fucking left. That's not determination. That's a person who has run out of road and turned around and tried the other direction because standing still wasn't working.

Bear received me when I was still mostly wreckage. Way later, I built his website.

I didn't know what I was doing. No Figma, no vocabulary for what the things were called, no framework for how the pieces connected. I was learning the way you learn when the credential path is closed — Loyola didn't accept me, CLC couldn't hold me, and I'd walked into the College of Lake County University Center looking for a door that turned out not to be open for someone with my transcript. So I sat down at a folding table and started clicking through things until they made sense, and then I took a screenshot so I'd remember where I'd been, and then I clicked through more things.

The screenshot wasn't a technique. It was the first prosthetic. My brain couldn't hold the state of what I was doing, so I made the screen remember instead.

That's the whole origin story. A person who couldn't trust their memory taking pictures of their screen.

It started with Lovable — an SVG image generator, print-on-demand, trying to get rich quick when AI started breaking open. I didn't get rich. The tool stayed anyway. Wrong reason, right direction. That's been the pattern the whole time: I show up with the wrong motive and end up somewhere useful. The get-rich-quick SVG business led to Claude. Claude led to a Node.js install I barely understood. The Node.js install led to the first thing I did with Claude Code, which was research AI certificates and degrees, which turned up one program at Loyola University, which I didn't qualify for. Which led me to CLC, where I talked to a counselor and then went to the University Center and looked at options and then got absorbed with Claude and never signed up.

The enrollment didn't happen because something else had already started pulling harder.

I couldn't tell you what the first thing I built was. The record exists in GitHub somewhere with a timestamp on it, not in my brain. The brain didn't keep it. The tools did. That's not a metaphor — my Recall Memory tested at the 12th percentile. The engine runs at full speed and the storage doesn't retrieve on command. Every repo, every screenshot, every note in every notebook on this table is the system doing the remembering my brain can't do. The tools I've catalogued aren't proof of productivity. They're evidence of injury. Each one marks a moment when I found out my brain couldn't be trusted with something, so I made an external thing hold it instead.

The table is where that's been happening. The same table, this whole time.

I'm writing this at it now.

That's the part that keeps stopping me when I get to it. Bear's website was built here. The learning cascade started here. FlashFusion started here. Chaos Club started here. The memoir is being written here. The surface hasn't changed. The construction that's been running on top of it — 240-plus configurations, 656 GitHub repos, the skills library, the audit scorecard, the three-screen setup with the RGB glow at 1:30am — all of it grew off a card table that cost forty dollars and was never supposed to be permanent.

It didn't fold. The work didn't stop. The table stayed.

Scene II — The Wall

The notes have been on the wall by my door for over a year.

Not stuck there temporarily. Not waiting to be filed somewhere better. Taped up with the specific permanence of things that are doing a job — the same job every day, every time I walk past, which is every time I leave and every time I come back. After a while you stop seeing them the way you stop seeing the light switch. They become part of the wall instead of a thing on the wall. The work they're doing is invisible from the inside. The work keeps getting done.

There are three layers.

My handwriting is in black marker, all-caps, on notebook paper. Three notes.

CHECK YOUR NOTEBOOK!!! YOU PROBABLY FORGOT SOMETHING.

Three exclamation marks. The yelling is at myself. Not a reminder — an order. The kind of thing you write when you've already forgotten enough things that polite language stopped working. There's something accurate about it. The notebook is the external storage. The note is the reminder that the external storage exists. The brain needs a note to remember the notebook exists because the brain is not reliable enough to remember the notebook without the note. The note is a workaround for a brain that needs a workaround for its own workaround.

CHANGE YOUR PATTERNS = CHANGE YOUR HABITS.

Written like an equation. Like I had to make it mathematical to believe it. The equal sign doing real work. If P then H. Change the inputs, change the outputs. I understand systems better than I understand myself, so I wrote the self-help line in the language of systems. The engineer's version of a mantra.

FOCUS ON THE SHIT THAT WORKS.

The most honest line on the wall. Not a meditation phrase, not a clinical recommendation, not something from a workbook. An order, in my register, in the only language I trusted enough to actually read at the moment I needed to read it. There are mornings that note has done more than an hour of therapy. You walk past and it stops you. You go back.

Then there's my mother's handwriting in purple marker, rounded, careful, ends in a smiley face.

All is well. Everything is working toward my highest good. Every encounter and every situation will only bring me good. I am safe. I am protected. I am LOVED ☺

Different voice. Different register entirely. The gap between my handwriting and hers is the gap between how I talk to myself and how she talks to me. My notes are commands. Hers are reassurances. My notes assume I've already failed to do something. Hers assume I'm going to be okay. I don't know how to write to myself the way she writes to me. So her handwriting stays on the wall and does it instead.

She wrote it over a year ago. I was still finding my way. That's the whole context. Not a crisis she was intervening in, not a recovery she was marking. A middle period — the continuous, ongoing middle of a person who is always still finding their way — and she wrote something into it that has been holding ever since. The note didn't stop being necessary. It stayed. The year accumulated around it.

Above all of this — both my handwriting and hers, both the orders and the reassurances — is a drawing.

Three big cats. A lion with a mane. A tiger with hearts on its face. A smaller one underneath, trailing the group. Yellow ground, blue and teal sky, a palm tree in the corner. Every animal has stitches drawn on it — little X marks across their bodies, the way a kid draws a scar. They're stitched up. They're smiling anyway.

Kinsley made it.

I don't know how old she was when she drew it. I don't know if she knew what she was making or if she was just drawing cats the way she draws cats. But the stitched animals smiling above the survival notes have been on the wall this whole time, and I have thought about them more times than I can count, and what I think when I look at them is: she drew the stitches and she drew the smiles and she put them on the same animals and she didn't think that was a contradiction.

The light switch is below all of it. Brass plate, toggle switch, the kind that's been there since the apartment was built. You flip it every time you leave, every time you come back. The wall holds Kinsley's tigers and my mother's purple ink and my own yelling-at-myself and the light switch below everything, and every day you pass through it twice.

I had a framed photograph of Kinsley on this wall too. Custom made. I had to take it down.

In the photograph she has her hand outstretched. Every time I looked at it I heard the same thing: Daddy, please help and save me. I don't know if that's what the photograph is. I don't know if that's what she'd say if she could see where I am. What I know is I couldn't be in the room with the sound of it, so I took the frame down and put it somewhere I don't pass every day. The drawing stays. The notes stay. The photograph is somewhere else.

There are things on the wall you keep because they hold you and things you take down because they break you open in a way that isn't useful, and the difference between those two categories is not always obvious from the outside. You have to live with the wall for a while before you know which is which.
