About

The path
wasn't linear.

Self-taught builder. Writer. Father. Currently building three things at once and writing about all of them in public.

I came up through the back of the house. I bartended at O'Toole's in my twenties. I worked in quality control. I taught myself to program in the margins of jobs that didn't require it. Somewhere in that arc — by injury, by choice, by circumstance, depending on which part of the story you ask about — the cognitive architecture I was born with stopped working the way it used to. Recovery assumes there's something to go back to. That wasn't the situation. So I started building instead.

The thing I noticed, the further I got into the rebuild, was that the diagnostic instinct I'd developed reading drunks at the bar was the same instinct that worked on processes in QC, and the same one that worked on systems in software. Same skill. Different substrates. The architecture broke. The instinct didn't. Everything I've built since is downstream of that single observation.

IWhat I do now

I work an L1 service desk by day at a managed IT firm. I'm not going to pretend that sentence does what people want a bio to do, which is establish credentials. It doesn't. It establishes where I show up at 8am, which is a different kind of fact. The work that sets the trajectory I'm actually pointed at happens in the margins — early mornings, late nights, the hours after my daughter goes to sleep on the Fridays I have her.

Three things, in parallel:

Each one is its own lane. They share a builder, a worldview, and a thesis — that you can build a functional self out of non-standard materials on a non-standard timeline — but they don't share an audience or a roadmap. Lane discipline matters. A lot of what I do is making sure the projects don't bleed into each other.

IIHow I got here

The short version is that I am 40 years old, and I spent most of my twenties and early thirties doing things that don't show up on a resume. The long version is in the memoir. The middle version, the one that fits in a paragraph, is this: I was the kid in PeeWee football who could read the offense. I was the bartender who could spot the table that was about to leave without paying. I was the QC tech who could feel when a process was drifting before the metrics caught up. The diagnostic instinct showed up early. I just spent a long time not knowing what it was for.

What I figured out — slowly, painfully, with help from people I'm still trying to repay — is that the instinct generalizes. It works on whatever substrate you point it at. The same skill that reads a room reads a system. The same skill that reads a system reads a codebase. The cognitive disruption that took out my memory and my ability to trust my own narrative didn't take out the diagnostic instinct. Whatever I am now is built on the one thing the damage left intact.

IIIWhy I write

I write because I can't lie. Not because of character, though I'd like to think that's part of it. Because of the injury. Half the days I can't remember what I had for lunch. Maintaining a false narrative across years, across testimonies, across the weight of the people in my life — it's structurally impossible for me. What's in this writing is what happened, what I'm doing, and what I think about it. That's not a selling point. It's just the only option available to me, and I've made peace with it.

I write because the next person coming up through a non-traditional path deserves the writing I didn't have. The security playbook is for them. The manifesto is for them. The book is for my daughter, but the rest of it — the public work — is for the version of me from twelve months ago, who would have shipped most of what I now warn against.

I write because building three things in parallel only works if you're documenting the throughline. Otherwise it's just busy. Writing is how I make sure the construction project has a spine.
