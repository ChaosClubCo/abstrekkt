# Construction, Not Recovery — Assembled Draft
## Working Title: The Technical Specifications
**Status:** Draft with additions — May 7, 2026
**Source:** Original prose (Kyle) + additions (Claude, voice-matched)
**Slot:** Likely Part III opener or standalone essay / could serve as prologue replacement

---

July 7, 2022. A 36-year-old bartender walked into a clinic in Bannockburn, Illinois, and sat still while a radioactive tracer mapped the blood flow in his brain. The intake form asked for occupation. He wrote: Bartender. Timothy O'Tools, Libertyville IL.

The scan took four days. He came back on July 11 for the second one.

The report came August 2.

---

I'll tell you what it showed because the numbers are the story, and I'm tired of telling the story without the numbers.

Processing Speed: 99th percentile.
Recall Memory: 12th percentile.
Resilience: 2nd percentile.
Flexibility: 17th percentile.
Stress Control: 27th percentile.

Engine at full speed. Storage almost empty. No brakes. Almost no capacity to bounce back when the system gets hit.

The radiologist's summary described what he saw as "evidence of past brain injury and encephalopathic process." He used the word scalloping — areas on the brain's surface where blood isn't flowing the way it should, consistent with toxic exposure and oxygen deprivation.

It looked like something had been eating at the outside of it for years.

It had.

---

There's something the report does that I didn't notice until I read it a second time. On page two, it dates the hypoxia event — the overdose that caused the oxygen deprivation — as "summer 2020." On page three, the same event is dated October 2021.

The physicians produced an unreliable narrator about the event they were documenting.

I'm not correcting it. I kept the contradiction because the clinical record demonstrating my memory problem is the clearest example I have of my memory problem. A 15-page neurological evaluation that can't agree with itself about when the injury happened. I gave them the intake interview. They wrote it down. Somewhere in the transfer, the timeline blurred.

That's the book I'm writing. Not about addiction. Not about recovery. About what happens to the record of a life when the person living it can't be trusted to remember it accurately. The Amen Clinics didn't catch the contradiction because I didn't catch it. We were both working from the same degraded source.

---

When I got that report, I was working at a bar. I had been sober from hard drugs for about ten months. I was living with my parents. My hope score — the eval actually measured hope, 64-point scale — came back at 27.

Readiness to Change: 10 out of 10.

That combination is what I want you to understand. Not optimism. Not rock bottom inspiration. Willingness without hope. I'll try anything because I have nothing left to protect, including the story I was telling about why the old way might still work.

The construction started from that.

---

The first thing I built wasn't software.

It was a website for a life coach in Sedona. His name is Soaring Bear. My mother found him the way mothers find things when they're running out of options — she found him because she had to. She paid for the trip. I got on a plane because I had already tried the other way, twice, and I still couldn't get out.

I built the site on a card table.

I want you to understand what that means spatially. Not a desk. A card table — the kind that folds in half and lives in a closet between family holidays. I set it up in a room at my parents' house and put a laptop on it and started watching YouTube videos about how websites work.

That's the origin story. Not a clean workstation. Not a mentor. Not a bootcamp. A folding table and a search bar and the specific stubbornness of someone who has nothing left to be precious about.

The screenshot method came out of that. I couldn't retain sequences. I still can't, not reliably. So I started screenshotting every step of every process — the button I clicked, the menu that appeared, the outcome. Not to document for someone else. To document for myself the next day, who would not remember I had done it.

The 12th percentile recall problem had a workaround. The workaround was: assume the memory won't hold and build the external record before you need it.

That worked. Imperfectly, inconsistently, and well enough to be dangerous. I kept going.

---

What I built after the scan wasn't a productivity system. I want to be clear about that because the vocabulary of tools and workflows has been colonized by people who are optimizing something that already works. That's not what I was doing.

Every external system I built after July 2022 was compensating for a specific gap the scan made legible. The documentation wasn't a habit. The note-taking wasn't a personality trait. I document everything because the internal system for storing things — 12th percentile recall — can't be trusted to hold them. Every note is a handoff from the brain I have to the brain I needed.

Not because I'm organized. Because I can't afford not to be.

By the time I understood what I was doing, I had built something I couldn't fully see. The screenshot habit became a documentation system. The documentation system became a tools architecture. The tools architecture became the operating system for a version of me that could execute at a level the scan said shouldn't be possible.

I've catalogued somewhere north of 240 configurations and tools at this point. I know about 30 of them well. The rest are the archaeological evidence of a process that's still ongoing — each one marking a moment when I discovered another function that had become unreliable and needed external scaffolding.

There's a daughter. She doesn't know any of this yet. That's part of what this is — an attempt to leave her a record that the internal system can't be trusted to hold. The book is the same project as the screenshot. Assume the memory won't hold. Build the external record before you need it.

Her name is Kinsley.

---

Memory doesn't fail gracefully. It doesn't give you an error code or a stack trace. One day you just reach for something that should be there — a name, a date, a sequence of events you lived through — and it isn't.

---

Recovery assumes there's a previous state to return to. I can't return to a baseline I can't verify. I don't know with confidence what the pre-injury version of my memory could hold, because the injury changed what I can access about the before.

So I didn't recover. I built.

The distinction matters. Recovery is a story about getting something back. Construction is a story about making something that never existed in the form you need. Different project. Different materials. Different definition of done.

The bartender in the intake form is still in there somewhere. What he didn't know yet — sitting in that chair while the tracer moved through his brain — was that the scan would give him the technical specifications for what needed to be built. That it would take three more years to understand what he was building. That he'd still be building.

That's not a recovery arc.

That's an architecture project with no scheduled completion date.
