Seven Things Worth Knowing About

Resources, ideas, and habits I'd point a younger version of me toward. None of these are on a "top tools" list. Most of them are dull. The dull ones compound.

Kyle Rosebrook · 6 min read

The "hidden gem" framing is usually a marketing move — packaging a thing as obscure to make it feel valuable. That isn't this. These are seven things I genuinely think are underrated relative to how useful they've been to me, and the reason most of them stay underrated is that they don't make for great content. They're slow. They're boring. They reward people who keep showing up, which is not how the algorithm rewards anyone.

I've ordered them roughly by how much they've changed how I work, lightest to heaviest. Number seven is the one that matters most.

Gem 01

Wiz Security's research blog

Resource · wiz.io

Wiz is the team that found the Moltbook breach within minutes of looking at client-side code. Their research blog is a free education in how AI app failures actually look from the outside, written by people who break things for a living.

It's not written for builders — it's written for security teams. That's what makes it useful. Reading the way attackers and auditors describe your kind of app is the fastest way to see what you're missing. I read every post. Most of them are about apps I'd otherwise have built the same way.

Why it matters

The breaches you're going to read about in the news next year are being described in research blogs this year. The lag is the moat — close it.

Gem 02

The fix-before-expand rule

Habit · personal rule

Before adding any new feature, asset, or tool to any project, finish what's already broken or partially shipped. That's the whole rule. Sounds obvious. Almost nobody does it, including me, until I started naming it.

The rule has saved me from at least three half-built products this year. Most "I'm overwhelmed" feelings are just unfinished work in disguise. The fix isn't a productivity system. The fix is closing one open loop before you open the next.

Why it matters

Builders ship more by saying no to themselves than by saying yes to anything new. The rule is the no.

Gem 03

The working file of facts

Habit · personal practice

One single document. Plain text or markdown. Contains every fact that's actually true about you, your work, your tools, your projects, your day-to-day. Updated as things change. Shared with whatever AI you work with so it can verify against it before drafting anything.

Mine is about 4,000 words. It includes my real job title, my real city, my actual tool counts, my real project status. Every public-facing draft gets checked against this file before it leaves the working session. The model can be fluent all it wants. The file gets the last vote.

Why it matters

The single biggest source of AI-assisted writing failure is fluent invention of plausible details. The file is the cure.

Gem 04

Reading your own cognitive assessment

Practice · self-knowledge

If you've ever taken a real cognitive assessment — a Total Brain, a neuropsych eval, even a structured ADHD evaluation — read it. Then re-read it. Then look at the systems you've built for yourself in life and notice how every one of them is a workaround for something specific that the assessment named.

I have one cognitive measure in the 99th percentile and another in the 12th. Knowing that explains every system I've ever built — and made me build better ones once I named the gap. Most people never look at their assessments. They take them, get a one-page summary, file it. The actual data underneath is the gem.

Why it matters

The system you build for yourself is only as honest as your understanding of what you're building it around. Most people are guessing.

Gem 05

The two-folder discipline

Habit · environment hygiene

Active project work goes in a folder that is not synced to any cloud provider. Reference material and finished archives go in a folder that is. The two never mix.

OneDrive, Dropbox, iCloud — they will fight your dev environment. node_modules uploads forever. Build outputs trigger sync storms. Git indexes get corrupted in interesting ways. I lost a full day to this twice before I learned the rule. Now I keep active code in D:\02_Homebase, off any sync provider. Boring. Saves hours weekly.

Why it matters

Most "my repo broke overnight" mysteries are environment hygiene problems wearing a costume. The folder is the costume.

Gem 06

The managed IT as classroom

Idea · career-shaped

This one's contrarian. Most career advice for self-taught builders treats the day job as the obstacle — the thing you have to escape so you can do the real work. I think it's the curriculum.

An managed IT is the highest-resolution dataset you can get on what humans actually need from the tools you're about to build. You see every confused user, every misconfigured setting, every error message that doesn't make sense, every workflow that broke for reasons the engineering team never imagined. The credentialed engineers building those tools rarely see any of it. If you're ever going to build for users, sit at the desk that fields their tickets first. The build will be better.

Why it matters

The room you're in is the room you're best equipped to build for. Don't leave too soon.

Gem 07

Writing things down for one specific person

Practice · the one that matters

Not for "an audience." Not for "the algorithm." Not for "future me." For one specific named person you can picture reading it.

The writing changes when you do this. Performance drops out. Hedge words drop out. The unnecessary throat-clearing at the start of every paragraph drops out. What's left is the actual thing you wanted to say to that person, written in a way that doesn't waste their time.

The book I'm writing has one reader. Everything else I write — essays, manifestos, even technical playbooks — has one reader too, just usually a different one. The discipline is the same. Pick the person before you start. Write to them. Whoever else reads it will know you weren't writing to them, and they'll respect the writing more for it.

Why it matters

Writing for everyone produces writing for no one. Specificity is the only universal that works.

The thing nobody talks about is usually the thing that compounds. Most of these will outlast every tool you're using right now.
