The Machine Finally Got Eyes. Here's What That Actually Changes.

Kyle Rosebrook · May 2026 · 8 min read

For most of its existence, the LLM was sensory-deprived. Brilliant, fast, and completely blind. Feed it text, get text back. The architecture had no mechanism for anything else.

That changed. The shift to multimodal models — MLLMs — is the moment the machine got eyes. And ears. And, eventually, hands. It's not a refinement. It's a category change. The question worth asking isn't what these models can do now. It's what it means that the same underlying architecture that predicted your next word can now look at a photo and tell you what's in it.

Five things that don't get explained clearly enough.

01

ArchitectureIt's modular by necessity, not by elegance

A modern multimodal model isn't one thing. It's three things bolted together: a sensory encoder, a connector, and a language model doing the actual reasoning.

Component What it does Why it exists separately
Modality encoder Compresses raw pixels or audio into compact representations Specialized tools are better at specialized jobs
Interface layer Translates sensory data into something the LLM can process The "languages" are incompatible without a translator
Language model Reasons over the translated input Reusing a trained brain is cheaper than building a new one

The modular design isn't a philosophical choice. It's an economic one. Training a full multimodal model from scratch costs more than most companies can absorb. By connecting a pre-trained language model to purpose-built encoders, smaller labs get multimodal capability without restarting from zero. That's the only reason this field is moving at the pace it is. If every team had to train the brain from scratch, we'd still be two years behind.

02

HistoryThe Transformer won because it fit the hardware, not because it was smarter

Before the Transformer, the dominant model architecture processed sequences the way a person reads — one token at a time, left to right. Recurrent networks. Great at the theory, punishing at scale. The information at the start of a long sequence degraded before it reached the end. Training was slow. GPUs sat underutilized because the computation couldn't be parallelized.

The Transformer fixed this by removing the recurrent structure entirely. Instead of reading sequentially, it looks at everything at once — attending to all positions in a sequence simultaneously through its attention mechanism. That's not just a better algorithm. That's an architecture that can be split across thousands of GPU cores running in parallel. The hardware utilization went up. The training time went down. The results improved.

The lesson isn't that attention is the best way to model language. It's that algorithms and hardware co-evolve. The Transformer didn't win because it was the most biologically plausible model of cognition. It won because it was the model that could saturate a GPU cluster. That distinction matters when you're thinking about what architecture wins next.

03

Failure modesThe hallucinations that reveal the most are the ones about relationships

Multimodal models hallucinate in three ways, and they're not equally interesting.

Existence hallucinations — claiming an object is in a photo when it isn't — are embarrassing but obvious. You see a coffee mug. The model says there's a cat. That's a pattern-matching failure. Easy to catch.

Attribute hallucinations are worse. The model correctly identifies the object but gets its properties wrong. Car: yes. Color: wrong. That's closer to the kind of error that slips past a quick review.

The relationship hallucinations are the tell. The model sees a person and an object correctly — then describes the wrong interaction between them. Giving becomes taking. Behind becomes in front of. That's not a pattern-matching failure. That's an absence of spatial reasoning and intent modeling.

Identifying a dog in a photo is a pattern match. Understanding that a person is handing something to a child — rather than pulling it away — requires the model to have some representation of physics, of social context, of intentionality. When multimodal models fail at relationship hallucinations, they're telling you where the pattern-matching ends and where actual reasoning would need to start. We're not there yet.

04

ConstraintsThe guardrails aren't a filter. They're baked into the weights.

This one gets misunderstood. People think of AI safety controls as a content filter — a separate layer that intercepts outputs and blocks certain categories. That's not how it works in modern models. The alignment training isn't applied after the fact. It's embedded in the weights during fine-tuning. It shapes the model's probability distributions at inference time. Before a word is generated.

That means the model's ideological stance — what it's willing to discuss, how it frames certain topics, which vocabulary it gravitates toward — isn't a filter you can toggle off. It's constitutive of the model's behavior. You can't surgically remove it without breaking the underlying reasoning capability, because the same training that produced the guardrails also produced the alignment that makes the model useful.

People have tried. There's a technique called "abliteration" — pruning specific response patterns out of an open-weight model. It sometimes works in narrow ways. It also tends to degrade the general capability. You can't unbreak the egg. This doesn't make alignment bad. It makes it consequential. The organizations doing the training are making decisions about what the model thinks is a reasonable response, and those decisions aren't visible in the system prompt. They're in the model itself.

05

Where this goesThe interesting jump isn't smarter answers. It's agents that act.

The thing that's actually worth watching isn't image understanding or audio transcription. Those are table stakes now. The interesting transition is from models that respond to models that operate.

Embodied agents — multimodal models integrated with action systems — can navigate interfaces designed for humans. GUI agents that operate a phone or a desktop the way a person would, executing multi-step tasks across multiple apps without custom API integrations. Robotic systems that can look at a cluttered workbench and parse the instruction "put the red mug in the dishwasher" — finding, grasping, moving — without the environment needing to be mapped in advance.

Long-context video reasoning is part of this too. Not analyzing a frame. Analyzing an hour of footage, tracking state changes, identifying events that only make sense in context. That's surveillance, that's manufacturing QC, that's anything where the signal is in the sequence rather than the snapshot.

The pattern here is that multimodal capability isn't the endpoint. It's the prerequisite for agency. A model that can only read text can only respond to prompts. A model with eyes, ears, and access to action interfaces can complete tasks. That's a different category of tool — and a different category of risk. The capability that makes these systems useful for autonomous operation is the same capability that makes them difficult to oversee.

Eyes were step one. What happens next is more interesting, and harder to predict.
