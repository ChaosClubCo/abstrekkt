# Rival AI workers unite to defend Anthropic against the Pentagon

**Thirty-seven researchers from OpenAI and Google DeepMind — fierce competitors of Anthropic — filed an extraordinary amicus brief on March 9, 2026, backing Anthropic's federal lawsuit against the Trump administration after the Pentagon branded it a national security supply-chain risk.** The designation, typically reserved for foreign adversaries like Huawei, was levied after Anthropic refused to let the military use its Claude AI for mass domestic surveillance or fully autonomous lethal weapons. The case has become a flashpoint for AI governance, testing whether the government can coerce private companies into stripping safety guardrails from frontier AI systems. It represents the first time a U.S. company has been publicly targeted with a supply-chain risk designation under federal procurement law.

## How a contract dispute escalated into a constitutional crisis

The conflict traces back to a **$200 million contract** Anthropic signed with the Department of Defense in July 2025, making Claude the first frontier AI model deployed on classified Pentagon networks. Claude was used in military operations including the raid that arrested Venezuelan leader Nicolás Maduro in January 2026 and in the ongoing conflict with Iran. But in late 2025, the DoD demanded Anthropic adopt an "all lawful use" framework, removing two non-negotiable restrictions: **no mass domestic surveillance** of Americans and **no fully autonomous lethal weapons** without human oversight. Anthropic refused.

On February 27, 2026, retaliation came swiftly from two directions. President Trump posted on Truth Social directing every federal agency to "IMMEDIATELY CEASE all use of Anthropic's technology," calling the company "Leftwing nut jobs." Within hours, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth ordered the Pentagon to designate Anthropic a "Supply-Chain Risk to National Security" under **10 U.S.C. § 3252** — a statute designed to protect against foreign adversaries who might sabotage military systems. Hegseth went further, directing that no contractor, supplier, or partner doing business with the U.S. military could conduct any commercial activity with Anthropic, with a six-month phase-out period. The designation was formally confirmed in a letter to Anthropic on March 4, 2026.

On March 9, Anthropic filed two federal lawsuits: one in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California and another in the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals. The 48-page California complaint alleges violations of the Administrative Procedure Act (the designation was "arbitrary, capricious, and contrary to law"), **First Amendment retaliation** for protected speech about AI safety, Fifth Amendment due process violations, and ultra vires executive action exceeding statutory authority. Anthropic has requested a temporary restraining order, with a hearing proposed for March 13, 2026.

## The amicus brief: competitors say "we will not be divided"

Filed hours after Anthropic's lawsuits, the amicus brief signed by **37 researchers and engineers** from OpenAI and Google DeepMind represents an extraordinary moment of cross-competitor solidarity. The most prominent signatory is **Jeff Dean**, Google DeepMind's chief scientist and leader of the Gemini AI program — one of the most influential figures in modern machine learning. Other named signatories include Google DeepMind researchers Zhengdong Wang, Alexander Matt Turner, and Noah Siegel, alongside OpenAI researchers Gabriel Wu, Pamela Mishkin, and Roman Novak. All signed in their personal capacities, not representing their employers.

The brief makes several forceful arguments. It calls the designation "an improper and arbitrary use of power" with "serious ramifications for our industry," warning that punishing a leading U.S. AI company "will undoubtedly have consequences for the United States' industrial and scientific competitiveness." The signatories argue the Pentagon had a far simpler remedy available — it could have "simply canceled the contract and purchased the services of another leading AI company" rather than weaponizing supply-chain authority. Most strikingly, employees of Anthropic's direct competitors endorsed its safety stance: "The best currently available AI systems cannot safely or reliably handle fully autonomous lethal targeting, and should not be available for domestic mass surveillance of the American people."

The brief also raises alarm about chilling effects. "It will chill open deliberation in our field about the risks and benefits of today's AI systems," the signatories wrote, adding that "in the absence of public law, the contractual and technological requirements that AI developers impose on the use of their systems represent a vital safeguard against their catastrophic misuse." By targeting Anthropic for maintaining safety guardrails, the brief argues, the government creates a perverse incentive for companies to avoid developing ethical restrictions at all.

## Legal experts predict the designation will collapse

The overwhelming consensus among legal scholars is that the government's position is legally untenable. Writing in Lawfare on March 2, University of Minnesota law professor Alan Z. Rozenshtein and co-author Michael Endrias called the designation "political theater: a show of force that was never meant to stick." They identified multiple fatal problems: the statute requires evidence of an "adversary" who may "sabotage, subvert, or maliciously introduce unwanted function" — criteria that plainly do not describe a domestic company refusing to remove safety features. Trump's own public statements calling Anthropic "RADICAL LEFT, WOKE" and Hegseth's characterization of the company's stance as "arrogance and betrayal" and "defective altruism" provide direct evidence of **viewpoint-based retaliation** rather than legitimate security concern.

A particularly damning logical contradiction undermines the government's case: the Pentagon simultaneously claims Claude is too dangerous to allow in the supply chain while continuing to use it in active military operations during the six-month phase-out, including in the Iran conflict. Just Security's Tess Bridgeman, a former Obama administration official, found it "highly unlikely" that Hegseth could meet the statutory requirements, and called the broad directive prohibiting all commercial activity with Anthropic "attempted corporate murder" that exceeds the statute's scope. The Lawfare analysis cited precedent from *Xiaomi Corp. v. DoD* (2021), where courts granted preliminary injunctions against unsupported DoD designations under similar circumstances.

## Industry reaction reveals unprecedented solidarity and internal tensions

The backlash against the designation has been sweeping. Before the amicus brief was filed, **over 300 Google employees and more than 60 OpenAI employees** signed an open letter titled "We Will Not Be Divided," urging their companies to support Anthropic's red lines. The Information Technology Industry Council, whose members include Nvidia, Amazon, Apple, Google, and OpenAI, sent a letter of concern to Hegseth on March 4. Additional letters went to Trump from the Software and Information Industry Association, TechNet, and the Computer and Communications Industry Association.

OpenAI CEO **Sam Altman** publicly broke with the administration, stating "enforcing the SCR designation on Anthropic would be very bad for our industry and our country" and calling it "a very bad decision from the DoW." This was notable because the Pentagon signed a deal with OpenAI within hours of designating Anthropic a supply-chain risk — timing Altman acknowledged "was definitely rushed, and the optics don't look good." The internal fallout at OpenAI was significant: alignment researcher Leo Gao publicly criticized the company's "all lawful purposes" agreement, and hardware/robotics leader **Caitlin Kalinowski resigned** over concerns about the Pentagon deal.

Consumer response ran strongly in Anthropic's favor. Claude surpassed ChatGPT in iPhone App Store downloads for the first time after the designation, with Anthropic reporting **more than one million new signups per day** as of March 5. Financially, while the designation threatens hundreds of millions in government and enterprise revenue — one partner already switched to a rival model, eliminating over $100 million in pipeline — Anthropic's DoD contract represented only about 1.4% of its projected **$14 billion** annual recurring revenue. The company recently closed a $30 billion funding round at a $380 billion valuation.

## What this case means for the future of AI governance

The case stands at the intersection of several unresolved questions about who controls frontier AI. As the amicus brief signatories wrote, "Until a legal framework exists to contain the risks of deploying frontier AI systems, the ethical commitments of AI developers — and their willingness to defend those commitments publicly — are not obstacles to good governance or innovation." With no comprehensive AI safety legislation in place, company-imposed restrictions are the primary mechanism preventing potential misuse of powerful systems.

The broader implications run in multiple directions. If the government can label any domestic AI company a supply-chain risk based on opaque or pretextual criteria, every AI firm negotiating government contracts faces existential uncertainty. Lawfare's analysis suggested the administration may be pursuing "something closer to the beginning of a partial nationalization of the AI industry" — using procurement power to dictate not just what the government buys, but how private technology operates. Defense procurement experts noted that the unprecedented secondary boycott — barring all military contractors from any commercial relationship with Anthropic — goes far beyond anything the statute was designed to authorize.

## Conclusion

As of March 10, 2026, the case is in its earliest hours. The government has not yet formally responded, and a hearing on Anthropic's temporary restraining order request is expected by March 13. But the legal and political dynamics are already clear. The cross-company amicus brief shattered the assumption that competitive rivalry would prevent the AI industry from collective action on safety. Legal experts nearly unanimously predict the designation cannot survive judicial scrutiny given the statute's plain language and the government's own contradictory statements. The deeper question — whether private AI companies retain the right to set safety boundaries on their own technology — will shape the relationship between government power and technological development for years to come. What started as a contract negotiation over usage terms has become the most significant legal test of AI governance in U.S. history.
