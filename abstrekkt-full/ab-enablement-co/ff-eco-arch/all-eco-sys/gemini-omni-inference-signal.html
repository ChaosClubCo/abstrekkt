AI Strategy

The inference gap is closing faster than anyone budgeted for

Gemini Omni isn't the story. What it signals about the direction of the whole category is.

By Kyle Rosebrook
June 2026

For the last two years, most of the capability conversation in AI has been about what models can produce. Output quality. Fidelity. Coherence. Whether the thing it generated looks like what you asked for.

That was the wrong frame. Or at least, it was the early frame.

The shift that's actually happening — and Gemini Omni is the clearest signal of it yet — is from generation to inference. The question is no longer "can it make a thing." It's "how much does it understand about the thing before it starts."

There's a difference between generating and understanding

And it matters for every system you're building right now

Early generative models were good pattern matchers. Feed them enough examples of X, they'd produce something that looked like X. The failure mode was legible: stylistically plausible, contextually hollow. You could tell the model didn't know what it was doing. It just knew what the output was supposed to look like.

What I'm watching happen in 2026 — across text, image, and now video — is models that appear to reason about context before acting on it. Not just "what does a video of this scene look like" but "what would actually happen in this scene, given what I know about physics, materials, and cause-and-effect."

The failure mode of early generative AI was plausibility without understanding. The thing that's changing is the gap between those two.

Gemini Omni is Google's video model, announced at I/O a few weeks ago. The framing they used was "anything from any input" — image, text, audio, existing video, all of it becomes a source you can pull from and combine into new output. Conversational editing on top of that. On paper, that's a capable production tool.

But the capability demonstrations I've seen aren't interesting because of what the model made. They're interesting because of how little it needed to make it.

Minimal input, maximal inference

This is the architectural shift that changes the cost model

The pattern showing up across the demos is consistent: sparse prompt, rich output that reflects domain knowledge the user didn't supply. Text descriptions of scientific concepts rendered accurately as animation. Static images extrapolated into motion with physically correct behavior. The model isn't just completing a pattern — it's drawing on knowledge about how things actually work to fill the gap between what you gave it and what the output requires.

Why this matters for builders
Every system you build on top of AI has a hidden assumption baked in: how much context does the model need from me to do this reliably? The more it needs, the more scaffolding you build — prompt engineering, retrieval, structured inputs, validation layers. If the model's inference quality improves enough, you stop needing scaffolding you wrote last year. That's not a feature update. That's an architectural shift in what your system needs to do.

I've been running automation and agent workflows for long enough to feel this in a concrete way. The scaffolding I wrote in 2024 was compensating for model gaps. Feed it the right context, constrain the output format, validate the result before passing it downstream. That's not bad engineering — it was the right call for the capability level at the time.

But if the model's baseline inference quality climbs high enough, some of that scaffolding becomes drag. You're solving a problem that got smaller. The ones who notice first get simpler systems. The ones who don't are maintaining complexity for a ceiling that moved.

The accuracy caveat is real and currently undersold

Confident output is not the same as correct output

There's a specific failure mode that gets worse as inference quality improves, not better. When a model looks uncertain, people check the output. When a model looks authoritative — when the output is polished, contextually fluent, and confident in its structure — people skip the verification step.

△
The fluency trap: Early demos of Gemini Omni's educational output show at least one factual error presented with the same visual confidence as the accurate material. The model doesn't flag its own uncertainty. It generates a complete, coherent result and lets you assume it's right. That's not a new problem — but it scales with how good the output looks.

This matters more for agentic deployments than for human-in-the-loop ones. If a video model generates an explainer and a person watches it before it goes anywhere, the fact-check happens. If that same model sits inside an automated content pipeline with no review gate, the error ships at the speed of the automation.

✓
The practical fix hasn't changed: domain review before deployment, built as a step in the pipeline rather than an afterthought. What changes is the stakes — because the output now looks good enough that skipping the step feels reasonable. That's when the step matters most.

What "inference quality" actually means for the automation layer

The part that doesn't show up in the demo reels

Here's the thing about a model that needs less from you: it also fails differently. When you over-scaffold a low-inference model, the failure modes are predictable. You've constrained the system enough that when it breaks, it breaks where you expected. You can write a test for that.

A high-inference model failing on sparse input fails in ways that are harder to anticipate. It made a reasonable-sounding inference that happened to be wrong. No format violation. No schema error. The output looks fine. The problem is upstream — in the interpretation the model made before it started generating.

That's a different quality problem. It requires different tooling. Evals over format validation. Semantic review over syntax checking. The shift in model capability doesn't eliminate the need for governance — it changes what governance has to look at.

For the practitioners building on this right now
Don't tear out your scaffolding yet. Audit it. Understand which pieces are compensating for model gaps vs. which pieces are doing real governance work. Some of what you built in 2024 is going to become unnecessary over the next 12 months. Some of it — the review gates, the human checkpoints, the semantic validators — becomes more important as the output gets more convincing. Know which is which before you start simplifying.

The pattern behind the announcement

Gemini Omni is the most visible example of a wider move

Google isn't the only one moving in this direction. The inference quality trend is showing up across every major lab, in every modality. The throughline is the same: models that require less explicit context to produce accurate, domain-appropriate output. The gap between "what I told it" and "what it needed to know" is narrowing.

For people building consulting practices, automation systems, or products on top of this infrastructure: the moat has never been the model. But what constitutes a useful layer on top of the model is shifting under your feet right now. The value of "I know how to prompt this" declines as the model needs less prompting. The value of "I know how to govern this, validate this, integrate this into a workflow that handles failure correctly" does not decline. It compounds.

The builders who get confused are the ones who thought the skill was the scaffolding. The ones who don't get confused understood that the scaffolding was always compensating for a temporary gap. Temporary gaps close.

Where this lands

Gemini Omni is a capable model and the inference quality is genuinely notable. But the more important thing it represents is a trend that's been building for 18 months and is now legible enough to plan around. Inference quality improves. Scaffolding requirements drop. Governance requirements don't. The systems that last are built around that order of operations, not against it.
