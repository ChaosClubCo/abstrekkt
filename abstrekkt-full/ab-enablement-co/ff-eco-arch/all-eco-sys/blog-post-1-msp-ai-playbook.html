MSP Strategy AI Reality Check May 2025

The MSP AI Playbook
Is Fiction

Not provocatively wrong. Just confidently, expensively, specifically wrong — and most MSP operators don't have the time to figure out where it breaks.

There's a version of the MSP AI strategy document circulating every consulting deck and LinkedIn thread right now. Four moves, always the same: build a multi-tenant data lakehouse, fine-tune an open-source model on your cross-client telemetry, layer agentic scaffolding into your RMM, and pivot to outcome-based pricing. Clean. Ambitious. Completely untethered from what a real MSP can execute next quarter.

I've read enough of these to know: the people writing them have never had to actually do it.

The Data Lakehouse Fantasy

Snowflake and Databricks are real products. They're also expensive, operationally complex, and require a data engineering function that most MSPs don't have — and couldn't justify hiring for before the revenue exists to pay for it.

The pitch is real: aggregate anonymized cross-tenant telemetry, use that signal as a competitive moat nobody can replicate. The problem is the pitch skips everything between "idea" and "running pipeline." You need data governance agreements with every client. Someone to build and maintain the ingestion layer. Enough normalized volume to make the downstream model work useful. A 51-to-200-person MSP doesn't have that. A 500-person shop might. Maybe.

What they actually mean

"Build a data lakehouse" is a 12-to-18-month infrastructure project at a well-funded organization with dedicated ML ops. As an MSP playbook step, it's a roadmap item for year three — dressed up as a quarter-one execution task.

The Fine-Tuning Fallacy

"Fine-tune Llama 3 for your client industry." Sure. That's GPU infrastructure, labeled training data, ongoing model ops, and someone who knows what they're doing with all three. When a consulting doc says this in one bullet point, what they mean is: eventually, a company with real ML resources will do this, and you should be aware it's possible.

What you should actually do right now: use existing frontier models via API and put your energy into the prompting, orchestration, and integration layer instead. That's the work that ships this year.

The Citation Problem

Source audit — from a real doc in circulation
Sources cited as evidence for autonomous OODA loop claims:
instagram.com  ·  mexc.com (crypto exchange)  ·  youtube.com  ·  linkedin.com

Not linked to specific content. Just the domain.

When the sourcing is that weak, the claims are weaker. Be skeptical of any AI strategy doc that lists a crypto exchange as a reference for cybersecurity architecture. What you're reading is generated confidence, not evidence-based analysis.

One doc I reviewed cited "OpenAI's Daybreak framework" as a real, named validation system for AI-generated patches. I cannot find evidence this exists. It reads like a hallucinated product name inserted to sound credible. That's not a small detail to get wrong.

What It Actually Gets Right

Not nothing. A few things hold up.

What's real

Trust and system permissions are the hardest moat. An MSP that has been inside a client's network for three years has something no AI startup can purchase. That's real and chronically undervalued.

Outcome-based pricing is the right direction. The hourly billing model degrades as automation scales. That's also real.

AI Governance-as-a-Service is a legitimate near-term wedge. Shadow AI is already a breach vector. Clients don't know what their employees are feeding into public models. Auditing that has real, near-term value.

The rest is aspirational architecture dressed as operational playbook. It's the difference between a strategy and a plan. One is a direction. The other requires a sequence of actual steps you can do with what you have right now.

The Honest Version

The companies winning at the MSP AI layer aren't building foundational models or data lakehouses. They're building execution scaffolding: the integrations, automations, and workflow orchestrations that let existing models act on real client environments.

They're winning on trust, on deep system access, and on the ability to ship something that actually works next quarter instead of promising something transformational in 18 months.

That's a smaller, more specific opportunity. It's also a real one. The playbook that says otherwise isn't lying exactly — it's just describing someone else's operation and handing it to you as instructions.
