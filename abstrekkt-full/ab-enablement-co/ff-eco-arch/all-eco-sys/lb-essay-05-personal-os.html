Personal OS: What I Actually Built

Most people post the polished version. Here's the honest version, including the parts that are blocked, overdue, and pending a bill I haven't paid yet.

Kyle Rosebrook · Load-Bearing · flashfusion.co

The personal operating system post is a genre now. Everyone has one. They all look the same: three layers, twelve tools, color-coded Notion setup, clean screenshots.

I've built something genuinely different from that — different in kind, not just in tool choices — but the honest version of it includes a Supabase bill that's been overdue long enough that the database is suspended, a Vercel GitHub App connection that broke and needs manual reconnect, and a skill library where four items can't deploy to the environment that matters most because of compliance gaps I haven't closed yet.

That's the real version. Here it is.

Why This Exists at All

I have a cognitive profile that makes certain things unreliable. Processing speed is fast — the information comes in. Retention is not. The information leaves before it can be used. The architecture I built is externalized cognition: moving memory out of my head and into systems that don't forget.

That's not a productivity framework. It's closer to a prosthetic. The distinction matters because prosthetics get built differently than optimizations. You're not making something faster. You're replacing something that was failing.

Everything below is built around that constraint.

What This Teaches About Personal OS

Every "personal OS" post I've read presents the system as if it's at steady state. The tooling is configured. The workflows run. The Notion database has no empty fields.

Steady state is aspirational. Real systems have blocked items. The discipline is naming them so they don't become invisible debt.

The most useful thing I do with my system isn't using the tools that work. It's maintaining a clear, accurate list of what's broken and why. The blocked items above are documented in a living file. Not in the back of my head — in a file with a name and a last-updated date.

That's what makes it a system instead of a collection of tools. The system includes the failure state. You know exactly what's broken, exactly why, and exactly what clears the blocker. Nothing is vaguely "coming soon."

The Supabase bill is $47. I know this. It's documented. It's not a mystery — it's a task.
