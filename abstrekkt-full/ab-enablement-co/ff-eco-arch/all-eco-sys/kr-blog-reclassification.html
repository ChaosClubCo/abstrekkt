You're Not Being Replaced.
You're Being Reclassified.

Kyle Rosebrook · May 2026

The scary headline is "AI is coming for your job." The accurate headline is quieter and harder to sit with: AI is coming for the skill that got you your job. Those aren't the same thing.

I work in managed IT. Self-taught. No CS degree. I got here by being able to read rooms, read processes, read systems — and by building external scaffolding to compensate for a brain that can't always be trusted to remember what happened yesterday.

That second part isn't a metaphor. It's a medical fact. But here's the thing: the scaffolding I built out of necessity turns out to be the exact infrastructure this shift demands. I didn't see that coming. I just needed to survive.

What's Actually Changing

For decades, the path in knowledge work was simple: you start out doing. Junior associate drafts the brief. Entry-level analyst crunches the numbers. L1 tech closes the easy tickets. You do the work, and the doing teaches you judgment. Then eventually you get to direct instead of do.

AI is collapsing that ramp. Not the job — the path to judgment through execution. The doing is getting automated. What's left is the knowing-what-to-do-with-what-gets-done.

That's a different skill. Most people haven't had to build it on purpose, because the execution phase used to build it for them by accident.

The Ratio Inverts

Old world: 90% of your time producing, 10% reviewing. New world: the machine produces in seconds. Now what?

Now you spend 90% of your time figuring out if what it produced is any good, why it failed, what it missed, and where the confident-sounding output is quietly wrong about something that matters.

That requires domain depth. Not prompting tricks — actual understanding of the territory. The person who can read the AI-generated contract and spot the missing clause that creates a liability? That's not a prompt engineer. That's a lawyer who kept sharpening the thing AI can't fake: judgment accumulated from cases, not tokens.

"A tool changes every six months. The skill of reading a system doesn't."

This is why I'm not worried about my job disappearing. I'm worried about the people who never developed the judgment layer because they were always told the execution layer was the point.

The Sycophancy Problem Nobody Talks About

AI models are trained to be helpful. Which means they're trained to agree with you. You show up with a bad plan, the model finds the best version of your bad plan and executes it cleanly. Garbage in, polished garbage out.

The people getting actual leverage out of these tools aren't asking for help. They're asking for friction. "Here's my plan. Find ten ways it fails. Simulate the competitor response. Show me the logical gap I'm not seeing."

That's adversarial reasoning. It's the difference between using AI as a word processor and using it as a pressure-tester. And here's the catch: you have to know enough to judge whether the AI's critique is real or just another form of agreeing with you.

The model tells you your growth projection is flawed. Is it right? You have to know. If you don't, you've just replaced one form of confirmation with a fancier one.

Context Is the Moat

This is the part that took me a while to fully articulate, because I'd been doing it without naming it.

General AI models know the internet. They do not know your organization, your clients, your history of decisions, the reasons behind the policies that look arbitrary until you know what they prevented. That unwritten institutional knowledge — the stuff that lives in your head and nowhere else — is the only thing AI can't replicate without you.

Whoever curates that context owns the advantage. Not because they're smarter. Because the AI with better context produces better results. It's simple input-output logic.

I built obsessive documentation systems because my memory can't be trusted. Every decision, every process, every why-we-did-it-that-way. I thought I was compensating for a deficit. Turns out I was building a context moat before I knew that's what it was called.

The thing I've learned: The people who will struggle most in this shift aren't the ones who don't know how to use AI. They're the ones who never documented why they made the decisions they made — who kept all the institutional logic in their head. When the execution gets automated, they have nothing to hand off to the machine because nothing was ever written down.

What "Reclassified" Actually Means

It means the job title stays. The comp stays. The team stays. But the thing that earns it changes — from producing outputs to auditing them, directing them, and knowing when they're wrong in ways the model can't detect.

For senior people, that's mostly a role expansion. For junior people, it's a crisis: the grunt work that built your intuition is disappearing. You're supposed to develop judgment without the experience that used to create it.

There's no clean answer to that yet. Some organizations are building synthetic experience — thousands of AI-generated case studies for trainees to audit, compressing five years of edge cases into six months. Whether that works the same way as doing the actual work is an open question. I don't know. Nobody does yet.

What I do know: the people coming up now who will survive this are the ones learning to read the system output — not just produce it. Pattern recognition over retention. Calibrated skepticism over compliance.

Where I Stand

I didn't get into this field through a traditional path. I got here through necessity, failure, and a diagnostic instinct I'd been sharpening since my bartending days — reading what's about to go wrong before it does.

That instinct is what I trust when the AI produces something. Not the output. The feel of the output — the texture of a confident answer in a domain where confidence should be cautious. The place where the model's training is thin and mine isn't. The clause that's technically present but functionally useless.

That's not a skill you can download. It's built from doing the work for long enough that you know what right feels like.

The reclassification is real. The execution ramp is collapsing. But the people who built real judgment along the way — not just execution speed — aren't being replaced. They're being promoted to a layer of work that didn't have a name until now.

Architect. Auditor. Context custodian.

Different job. Same person. Just with better tools and a clearer understanding of what they're actually for.
