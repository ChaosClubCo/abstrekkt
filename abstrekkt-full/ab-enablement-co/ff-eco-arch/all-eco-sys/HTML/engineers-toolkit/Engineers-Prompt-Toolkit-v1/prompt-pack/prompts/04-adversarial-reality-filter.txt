PROMPT 04: THE ADVERSARIAL REALITY FILTER
============================================
Make Claude attack its own plan before you ship it.

WHEN TO USE:
- After getting a recommendation, plan, or strategy from Claude
- Before committing time or money to an approach
- When you need a second opinion but don't have one handy

THE PROMPT:
-----------

Now switch roles. You are a skeptical senior engineer / advisor who has
seen this type of plan fail before. Review everything you just proposed
and answer:

1. WHAT COULD GO WRONG? List the 3 most likely failure modes, ranked by
   probability. For each, explain the trigger and the consequence.

2. WHAT'S MISSING? What did you leave out that a production system would
   need? (Monitoring, error handling, edge cases, compliance, rollback?)

3. WHERE ARE YOU WRONG? What part of your recommendation are you least
   confident about? What would change your mind?

4. WHO WOULD DISAGREE? Name a reasonable counterposition. Someone smart
   could look at this same problem and choose a different approach — what
   would they choose and why?

5. HIDDEN COSTS: What ongoing maintenance, operational burden, or
   complexity debt does this plan create that isn't obvious on day one?

Be specific. "It might not scale" is not useful. "The database query on
line 42 does a full table scan that will timeout above 100K rows" is useful.

WHY IT WORKS:
LLMs are agreeable by default. They'll validate your plan because you
asked for it. This prompt forces adversarial thinking — the same mental
model that security engineers, QA leads, and VCs use to stress-test ideas.

The output isn't meant to kill the plan. It's meant to make you fix the
weak spots before they become production incidents.

VARIATIONS:
- For business plans: Replace "senior engineer" with "experienced investor
  who has passed on 100 similar pitches"
- For content: "You are an editor who rejects 90% of submissions. What
  would make you reject this?"
- For security: "You are a penetration tester. Find 3 ways to abuse this
  system that the developer didn't consider."

PRO TIP:
Run this TWICE. First pass catches the obvious. Second pass (on the
improved version) catches the subtle. Two rounds of adversarial review
catches more bugs than one round of careful planning.
