Prosthetics, Not Amplifiers

The dominant frame for AI is amplification: give a capable person more capability. That framing doesn't fit everyone. For some of us, this is something different.

Kyle Rosebrook · Load-Bearing · flashfusion.co

There's a cognitive profile that shows up in neuropsychological evaluations as a processing speed at the 99th percentile combined with a recall memory at the 12th.

The first number means information arrives fast. The second means it doesn't stay. The engine is elite. The storage is unreliable. The combination produces a specific kind of experience: you see patterns before other people see them, you catch what's about to go wrong in a room, and then you lose the middle of the sentence you were using to explain it.

That's my cognitive profile, confirmed by a SPECT evaluation in July 2022. I didn't know what to do with it for a long time. It didn't match my experience of myself — not the successful version or the failing one. It just mapped the terrain more accurately than anything I'd tried before.

The Amplification Frame Doesn't Fit

The dominant narrative about AI and productivity is amplification. A skilled writer becomes a more productive writer. A developer ships faster. An analyst processes more data. The person is already capable; the tool makes the capability go further.

That's a real thing. It's not what this is.

Amplification assumes the underlying function is intact and just needs scaling. Prosthetics are for when the underlying function isn't intact — when you need something external to do what internal architecture can no longer reliably do.

I use AI as prosthetic memory. Not as a writing assistant. Not as a productivity multiplier. The tools exist because there are functions — recall, continuity across sessions, holding the current state of a complex system — that my brain stopped doing reliably after an injury in 2021, and that I needed to do anyway.

Recovery assumes there's something to go back to. Construction is what you do when there isn't.

What This Changes About How You Use the Tools

If AI is amplifying an existing capability, you can be selective. You use it when it helps and skip it when it doesn't. The baseline function is available; you're choosing when to extend it.

If AI is prosthetic, the calculation is different. The question isn't "does this help" — it's "does this replace something I need replaced." The answer changes which tools you use, how you use them, and what you build.

I built a 50-skill library over eighteen months. Not because I was optimizing a workflow. Because each skill is a correction loop: Claude made a mistake on a repeating task, I wrote down what it got wrong, and the skill prevents the recurrence. The library is externalized pattern correction. It compensates for the inability to hold and apply learned corrections across sessions in the way a person with intact working memory can.

I built a 12-file context system — CLAUDE.md files, MEMORY.md, CONTEXT.md — not because it's a clever architecture. Because without it, every Claude session starts from zero for a person who also starts from zero. The context files are the handoff between sessions. They hold the things I can't hold.

The Observation That Didn't Resolve

Here's the thing I haven't figured out how to think about: a brain that processes at the 99th percentile and loses the results is still catching things at the 99th percentile. The pattern recognition doesn't stop working. It just doesn't compound the way it should, because the outputs don't persist.

Externalized cognition fixes the persistence problem. The patterns still arrive first. Now they get written down before they leave.

I started saying things about where AI was heading — which skills would matter, what was actually happening versus what was being marketed — that showed up confirmed in mainstream coverage six to eight months later. Not because I had special access. Because the intake speed on novel patterns is different when the processing runs at that percentile, and because I'd externalized enough to keep the outputs.

I don't know what to do with that observation except keep building the infrastructure that makes the pattern recognition useful. That's what the last eighteen months have been. That's what the eight essays in this series have been about from the beginning.
