HR Can Measure Your Skills.
It Can't Measure What You're Carrying.

Skills intelligence frameworks are getting smarter. They still have a massive blind spot — and it's the layer that actually determines whether any of it works.

Kyle Rosebrook · Abstrekkt · 6 min read

Forbes published 13 strategies this week for identifying emergent skills before they become shortages.

The list is thoughtful. Map workflow changes quarterly. Track stretch assignments. Analyze recognition data. Tag projects with skill signals. Build dynamic skills intelligence systems. Reverse-engineer talent strategy from where you're headed in 18 months.

Not one of the 13 accounts for whether the person with the skills is okay.

Not one asks: Is this employee in a place where growth is even possible right now? Are they managing something nobody at work knows about? Are they running on fumes and calling it fine because calling it something else feels dangerous?

Skills can be mapped. Context can't.

The Assumption Underneath Every Skills Framework

Every skills intelligence approach — even the sophisticated ones — operates on an invisible assumption: the person is at baseline. That they have the cognitive bandwidth to learn, the emotional bandwidth to stretch, the stability to develop adjacent capabilities on top of their current load.

It assumes the person is ready.

A lot of people aren't ready. Not because they lack capability — but because they're in the middle of something.

A diagnosis. A custody situation. A loss. A slow-building mental health crisis they haven't named yet. A year of just barely keeping it together that looks fine from the outside.

No skills taxonomy captures that. No quarterly workforce planning cycle catches it. No AI-powered skills sensing layer reads it. Because it doesn't live in the data.

It lives in people.

The Gap That Doesn't Have a Job Description

One strategy in that Forbes list stood out: "Look beyond current roles and assess employee skill interest." Do quarterly assessments. Find out what employees want to develop.

It's the right instinct. Most organizations don't do it well. And even the ones that do it well can't hear what doesn't get said in a quarterly check-in.

What doesn't get said:

I'm interested in developing this capability, but I'm three months into a depressive episode and I can barely get through the work I already have.

I want to grow in this role, but my kid's struggling and I'm running on three hours of sleep and I don't have room for one more thing right now.

I'd love to take that stretch assignment. I'm also scared to admit I'm not holding it together the way you think I am.

I have the skills. I just don't have anything left to apply them with.

The chaos underneath skills development is invisible to HR. And it's not supposed to be HR's job to see it. That's not a criticism — it's a systems problem. The org has legitimate performance concerns. The employee has legitimate human concerns. Those two things don't coexist comfortably in a 1:1 or a quarterly skills assessment.

Somewhere, someone needs to create a space where that context can exist without making someone less hireable, less promotable, less safe to admit what's actually happening.

Not a Skills Problem. A Human Capacity Problem.

You can identify every emerging capability. You can build every reskill pathway. You can run the most sophisticated talent intelligence system in your industry.

And the person sitting in front of it might still be in the kind of chaos that makes growth feel like a foreign language.

That's not a character flaw. That's not a failure of ambition. That's just what it looks like when a human being is in a period they can't describe in professional terms — and the system they work inside has no room for that description anyway.

Chaos is normal. The part that damages people is pretending it isn't — especially in professional contexts where the cost of admitting it feels too high.

This is what the skills conversation almost never touches. The Forbes list of 13 strategies is well-researched and practically useful. It maps skills. It doesn't map the human underneath them.
