Your AI stack has a carrying cost. Most people don't know what it is.

The pitch was productivity. The reality is a subscription portfolio that grows by one tool every time someone reads a newsletter. Here's the actual math on what a typical SMB AI stack costs — not the list price, the real cost — and the three questions that determine whether any of it is worth keeping.

Kyle Rosebrook FlashFusion Tested in production

In the last two years, the average knowledge worker at a small business has accumulated somewhere between eight and fifteen AI-adjacent subscriptions. Some were purchased intentionally. Most were acquired the same way a drawer fills with takeout menus — one at a time, each one reasonable in isolation, none of them audited as a collection.

ChatGPT Plus because it was the obvious first move. Claude Pro because someone said it was better for writing. Cursor or GitHub Copilot because the developers asked. Notion AI because it was an upsell on something you already paid for. Perplexity because a podcast host mentioned it. A Zapier plan that's technically active. An n8n cloud instance someone set up in February and used twice. A Midjourney subscription from when everyone was doing AI images.

None of this is irrational. Each decision made sense at the time. The problem is the stack was never designed. It accumulated. And accumulated stacks have a carrying cost that nobody calculated because nobody was looking at the total.

That range is not a mistake. The low end is a disciplined stack with minimal seat duplication. The high end is what happens when procurement isn't centralized and every department lead has a credit card. Most businesses I've looked at are somewhere in the $500–700/month range — not because they're reckless, but because each line item passed a local cost-benefit check that nobody ran against the total.

The number nobody calculates: time cost

The subscription line items are the visible part. The invisible part is what the stack costs in attention.

Every tool in the stack has a context-switching cost. The moment you open a second AI interface because "this one is better for this task," you're paying a switching cost that doesn't appear on any invoice. Every tool that requires a prompt to be re-explained from scratch because there's no persistent context costs minutes per use. Every workflow that was automated in one tool but breaks when that tool's API changes costs hours to debug.

A rough model: if each tool in a 10-tool stack consumes an average of 30 minutes per week in maintenance, re-prompting, and context-switching, that's 5 hours per week across the stack. At a $75/hr blended cost for the people touching it, that's $375/week — $19,500/year — in time that doesn't appear in the AI budget but belongs there.

The carrying cost formula

True AI stack cost = subscription fees + (average weekly maintenance hours per tool × tool count × weeks/year × blended hourly rate). Most organizations track the first term and ignore the second. The second term is usually larger.

This is not an argument against AI tooling. It's an argument against unaudited AI tooling. The difference matters.

The three questions that determine whether a tool earns its keep

I've run versions of this audit on my own stack and on client stacks. The tools that survive it answer yes to at least two of the three questions. The tools that fail it consistently are tools where one person made the purchase and nobody ever checked in.

The load-bearing test — three questions per tool
Question 1: Does at least one person use this tool at least three times per week, without being prompted to?
If yes: the tool has active users. If no: the tool has subscribers. Those are different things. A tool with subscribers but no active users is a subscription you've decided is worth keeping without evidence.
Question 2: If this tool disappeared tomorrow, would someone notice within 24 hours?
The "remove it and wait" test. Tools that fail this question are tools that have been psychologically load-bearing (it's there when I need it) without being operationally load-bearing (I actually need it regularly). These are the easiest cuts.
Question 3: Does this tool do something that no other tool in the stack already does?
Most stacks have at least three tools that do substantially the same thing — usually a ChatGPT subscription, a Claude subscription, and something that bundles an LLM as a feature (Notion AI, Grammarly, a CRM's "AI writing assistant"). If the answer is no, you're paying for coverage, not capability.

Run this across every line item. A tool that fails all three questions is dead weight. A tool that fails two out of three is a candidate for consolidation. A tool that passes all three is load-bearing — it's earning its place in the stack.

Where the real consolidation opportunity is

The most common finding when I run this audit: the stack contains two or three redundant LLM interfaces and zero workflow automation. Teams have paid for the ability to prompt multiple AI models — which is fine — but haven't paid for the infrastructure that would let those models do useful work without a human initiating every single request.

The ROI math flips when you move from reactive AI use (I open the tool, I prompt it, I use the output) to proactive AI use (a trigger fires, the tool does work, I review the result). A $50/month n8n plan that runs one automated workflow five days a week produces more time savings than a $300/month seat portfolio of tools that people open when they remember to. The problem is reactive use is visible and proactive use requires setup.

Reactive use is visible. Proactive use requires setup. Most stacks are entirely reactive because setup was never prioritized over subscriptions.

The audit isn't just about cutting — it's about identifying where reallocation creates more value than elimination. In most stacks I've looked at, cutting two redundant LLM seats and redirecting that $50–100/month toward a properly configured automation layer produces a larger productivity gain than any of the individual tools it replaced.

What the honest ROI case looks like

AI vendors publish ROI figures with the rigor of a press release. The honest version is harder to calculate but more useful.

The actual payback question is: how many hours per month does this tool save, at what blended cost rate, against its monthly subscription? A tool that costs $30/month and saves two hours per month at a $50/hr blended rate returns $100 in saved labor against $30 in subscription cost — a 3.3x return. That's a tool worth keeping.

A tool that costs $25/month and saves 20 minutes per week — 80 minutes per month — at a $50/hr rate returns $67 against $25. Still positive, but barely. If there's a free tier that covers 80% of the use case, the paid tier is hard to justify on hours alone. It might be justified on reliability or features, but the math needs to be made explicit.

A tool that costs $99/month and mostly gets used when someone remembers it exists — maybe two hours of actual use per month — is returning $100 against $99. Not a bad absolute return, but the same two hours of intentional use of a tool you already pay for would likely produce the same outcome. The $99 tool isn't load-bearing. It's comfortable.

The payback calculation

Monthly ROI = (hours saved per month × blended hourly rate) ÷ monthly subscription cost. Anything below 2x is a candidate for audit. Anything below 1x is a candidate for cancellation. Most teams have never run this calculation on any line item in their AI stack.

Running the audit without making it a project

The reason stacks don't get audited isn't that the math is hard — it's that nobody owns it. The person who bought Perplexity isn't the person who bought Notion AI. The automation tool was set up by someone who's since left. The Midjourney subscription is on the CEO's personal card because it was faster to set it up that way in 2023 and nobody changed it.

The fastest version of this audit: open your company credit card statement and your personal credit card statement. Find every line item that contains a tool name you recognize as AI-adjacent. List them. For each one, answer the three questions above. Give yourself 90 minutes. You will find at least two tools that fail all three questions. Cancel those two tools today. That's the audit.

The slower version — the one that produces a real answer about what your stack should look like — requires mapping what work each tool is actually being used for, identifying where there's overlap, and deciding whether your automation layer is an actual layer or just a theoretical one.
