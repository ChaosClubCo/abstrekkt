Firing Myself Is the Product

Most consulting is built to keep you needing the consultant. Mine measures success by how fast you stop.

Most people selling this kind of work tell you what they're worth. I tell you when I'm done. Done means the person I trained can build something I didn't design, answer a question I was never asked, and explain the logic to someone else without calling me first. Done means I'm no longer needed. That's the actual product.

It's a strange thing to lead with when you're trying to get hired. Worth saying anyway, because the strangeness is the point.

The Dependency Trap

Most consulting is billed by time — the hour, the month, the project — which means the income scales with how much someone keeps needing you. Nobody has to plan it that way for it to work that way. The version of me that gets someone to self-sufficiency fast earns less per relationship than the version that keeps finding new problems as fast as the old ones close. That incentive runs underneath the relationship whether anyone says it out loud or not — shaping what gets handed off, what stays in someone's head, when an engagement is actually finished versus when it "needs another month."

I built the other version on purpose. Income comes from clean exits that produce something I can point to later, not from stretching an engagement past the point it needed to end. What gets documented is the deliverable, not a thing I quietly keep so the relationship has to continue. Someone else's independence is the proof the model works, not a threat to my income.

Why this is worth trusting before you've seen it work

I'm not paid more the longer someone needs me. That's not a pitch. It's the actual incentive running underneath the model — the same one I'd want if I were the one hiring.

What Done Actually Looks Like

Most engagements close on a date. Mine close on three things happening, regardless of how long they took to show up.

They adapt without calling. Something comes up that wasn't in the training — a case that doesn't fit the pattern, an output that looks wrong for a new reason — and they work through it themselves. The first time that happens without a call to me, something real has already changed.

They teach someone else without being asked. Not repeating a procedure — explaining the logic underneath it, what to do when the output looks unexpected. That's the moment the knowledge stops being mine and starts being theirs.

The calls change shape. Early on, it's "what do I do here." Later, it's "I tried this, here's what happened, does this track." That shift, from asking for the answer to asking for confirmation, is the actual signal. They've stopped learning from me and started thinking alongside me. That's the exit.

All three can happen inside a month with the right starting point, longer with a harder one. The calendar was never the measure. The outcomes are.

After the Exit

Once it closes, the capability stays without me. That's a different relationship than most consulting produces — most of it ends with the client still needing the consultant's knowledge, which turns the ongoing payment into something structural, even if nobody built it that way on purpose.

What I'm actually working toward, in order: someone who owns the capability, something I can show the next person considering this, and a referral to someone with the same problem. Staying on is a distant fourth. It only makes sense if they've outgrown what we built together and want to keep building on their own terms.

The version of this worth hiring is the one built to make itself unnecessary fastest. If that sounds backwards, it's worth asking why the other version doesn't.
