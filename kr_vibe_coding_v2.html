Vibe Coding Isn't What You Think It Is

The debate has been running since February 2025. Most of it is about the wrong thing.

Kyle Rosebrook
TL;DR
  • Karpathy's original "vibe coding" meant deliberate surrender of oversight — not AI-assisted development.
  • Most of what people call vibe coding is just faster tooling. That's not the same thing.
  • The actual question: at what layer of your stack does delegation stop being safe?
  • The hangover isn't from vibe coding. It's from applying prototype methodology to production systems.

The debate is about the wrong thing

The discourse around vibe coding is mostly people arguing past each other. One camp says it's irresponsible — you're shipping code you don't understand, no audit trail, no maintainability. The other camp says stop gatekeeping, it's just AI-assisted development, everyone's doing it.

Both sides are missing the point because they're arguing about a term that lost its definition six months after it was coined.

What Karpathy actually said

Andrej Karpathy introduced "vibe coding" on February 2, 2025. His framing was precise: "you fully give in to the vibes, embrace exponentials, and forget that the code even exists." He wasn't describing a workflow. He was describing a deliberate surrender of oversight. Accept all diffs. Don't read them. Paste error messages back in. Let the codebase grow past your comprehension.

That's vibe coding. What most people are doing with Cursor or Claude is not that. It's AI-assisted development — using a faster tool while still reviewing, still testing, still owning what ships. Calling that vibe coding is like calling a power drill carpentry.

The real question

The conflation matters because it papers over what actually needs answering: at what layer of the stack does it make sense to stop reading the code?

There's a useful answer. Features that nothing else depends on — isolated UI modules, throwaway prototypes, end-node functionality — those you can let run. If it's wrong, you rip it out. No downstream damage. The core architecture, the auth layer, the data model — that's where delegation gets expensive fast.

"The code that breaks in production was written by someone who thought the tool was the decision."

I've shipped things without reading every function. I've also spent whole afternoons unwinding agentic drift in places I should have been more present. The moments that cost me were always in the trunk. Not the leaves.

The hangover

By September 2025, Fast Company was covering the "vibe coding hangover" — senior engineers spending their days cleaning up AI-generated production code. Developers on LinkedIn had started renaming themselves "Vibe Code Cleanup Specialists." That's not a joke. It's a job description that didn't exist twelve months earlier.

The hangover isn't because vibe coding is wrong. It's because people applied a prototype methodology to production systems and were surprised when it behaved like a prototype.

Collins named it Word of the Year for 2025. That means it's mainstream. It also means the precision is gone. When a term goes mainstream, the nuance goes with it.

What actually works

The mental model matters more than the tools. Cursor, Claude, Copilot — the tool is not the variable. Whether you understand where you're delegating and where you're staying in the loop is the variable. Most people treating vibe coding as a general workflow haven't answered that question. They've removed a friction layer and called it a method.

There's a version of this that works: pre-plan before you generate, contain experiments to features with no downstream dependencies, verify at the output level rather than the line level, and know exactly where you can't afford to be wrong. That's not vibe coding as Karpathy meant it. It's something more disciplined — and more useful.

The vibes are fine. The question is whether you know what you're handing off.
