You Hired Five People to Do One Job

AI didn't change what needs to get done. It changed how many people you need to do it.

By Kyle Rosebrook June 2026 Topic Team Structure

Every company I talk to structures AI adoption like it's a transformation project. Committees. Pilot programs. A dedicated budget line for "AI enablement." Change management consultants explaining what AI is to people who already use it at home.

The companies that are actually getting leverage look different. Small teams. Senior-heavy. One person doing the work that used to take five.

There's a name for this structure in management consulting. The Obelisk model. I'd never use that word with a client. But the shape it describes is exactly what I'm installing when FlashFusion works.

What the old structure actually looks like

The traditional org chart for knowledge work is a pyramid. Broad base of junior people doing manual data work — research, formatting, synthesis, first drafts. A middle layer that reviews, consolidates, and packages. Senior people at the top who interpret, decide, and own the client relationship.

The base exists because the work is volume-intensive and repeatable. You need bodies to move information through the system.

The problem: repeatable information work is exactly what AI does. The same standardization that justified hiring a base of junior researchers is the same standardization that makes their tasks automatable. Not theoretically. Right now.

The pyramid base existed because the work was high-volume and repeatable. AI handles high-volume repeatable work by design.

This doesn't mean you fire the junior people. It means the junior people who adapt become mid-level. The mid-level people who adapt become senior. The org gets taller and leaner. Fewer people, each doing more leveraged work.

That's the obelisk shape. Taller, narrower, same total output — or more.

What the champion model is actually doing

When FlashFusion goes into a client org, I'm not installing software. I'm building a person.

One domain expert — someone who already understands the work, the clients, the context — learns to run AI as the execution layer. They stop doing the repeatable parts manually. They start using that recovered capacity for higher-leverage judgment calls.

That person becomes the internal AI champion. The org doesn't get dependent on an external vendor. It gets a new internal capability that compounds over time.

Three types of champion show up across every engagement I've run:

Type 1
The Explorer
Finds use cases fast. Comfortable with breaking things. Runs the experiments the rest of the team won't try. Best in early-stage adoption.
Type 2
The Translator
Makes AI accessible to colleagues. Builds templates, removes jargon, lowers the barrier for the rest of the team. Best at scaling what the Explorer found.
Type 3
The Strategist
Connects AI work to business outcomes. Speaks in ROI and KPIs. Makes the case internally. Best when adoption needs executive air cover.

Most clients have one of the three naturally. The engagement is partly about identifying which one you have, partly about teaching them to fill the gaps of the other two.

What changes and what doesn't

The work that changes: anything repeatable, high-volume, digital. Research. First drafts. Data formatting. Tier-1 triage. Scheduling logic. All of it becomes automatable with the right configuration. The champion handles the configuration. The AI handles the execution.

The work that doesn't change: judgment, relationships, accountability, context that lives in the room rather than in a document. AI can prepare the inputs. Humans still own the outputs that matter.

What also doesn't change: the need for someone to keep the system running. An AI workflow without a human steward degrades. The champion is that steward. They're not less important than they were. They're more important — because now they're responsible for a system that touches more of the organization's work.

Why five people became one

Not because the five people weren't good. Because three of them were doing work that's now in the automation layer. The one remaining person runs the automation layer and focuses the remaining capacity on the things that require a human.

The math isn't "we need fewer people." The math is "we can do more with the same people if we stop asking them to do machine work."

The obelisk isn't about cuts. It's about leverage. Every person in the org doing work at the level their judgment actually warrants — not one level below it because the volume-intensive layer is still being done manually.

The champion model is the obelisk model at micro-scale.

One trained person, elevated. One set of workflows automated. One org that doesn't need a vendor to keep running after FlashFusion exits.

That's the shape. Everything else is implementation detail.
