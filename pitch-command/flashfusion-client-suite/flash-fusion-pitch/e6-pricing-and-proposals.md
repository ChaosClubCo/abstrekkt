## Kyle's Teaching Notes (Pre-Read Before Session)

### Why pricing needs its own session

Most salespeople either underprice (because they don't believe in the value) or overcomplicate (because they don't understand the deal structure). Kelan's background at Silverfort means he's sold complex, high-ticket security software. That's an asset — use it.

The key reframe for this session: **we're not selling software licenses, we're selling a compressing-timeline on a business outcome the buyer was going to pursue anyway.** They were going to hire consultants, build something in-house, or fail their next audit. We're the faster, cheaper, safer path.

### The deal structure (what we're actually selling)

**Phase 1 — POC (Proof of Concept)**
- Duration: 4–8 weeks
- Deliverable: One working agent deployed in the customer's environment, with live audit trail and at least one ComplianceReport generated
- Price: $15,000–$25,000
- Goal: Prove the platform works in their environment. Generate internal buy-in. Create the paper trail for expansion.

**Phase 2 — Platform Subscription**
- Begins after POC sign-off
- Annual contract
- Price: $48,000–$120,000/year depending on agents, users, and integrations
- Y1 target: Close 3–5 customers at $60K–$100K average = $300K–$500K Y1

**Total deal anatomy (typical):**
- POC: $20K
- Y1 platform: $72K
- Total Year 1 value: $92K per customer
- At 4 customers: $368K Y1

This is the $300K–$500K Y1 target. 4–5 deals.

### How to present price without apologizing

The worst thing a salesperson can do is introduce price with a hedge: "So it's... kind of around... like $20K-ish for the POC..."

The correct delivery: "The POC engagement is $20,000 over 6 weeks. That gets you a live agent in your environment, a full audit trail, and a ComplianceReport you can use with your auditors."

Then stop. Don't add "which I know might seem like a lot" or "but we can look at different options." Let the price land. Wait for their reaction. Their reaction tells you where to go next.

### The value story (what justifies the price)

Every price conversation needs a value anchor. For FlashFusion:

- Manual compliance documentation: 2–3 weeks of staff time per audit cycle. At fully-loaded cost, that's $15K–$30K per audit. We automate that in 30 seconds.
- Failed audit: $50K–$500K in remediation costs, delays, and penalties depending on framework. We reduce that risk.
- AI pilot that died in security review: sunk cost of 3–6 months of engineering time. We compress that to a day.
- In-house build: $200K–$500K in engineering time over 12 months, with no compliance layer. We deliver faster, cheaper, and with the audit infrastructure included.

Pick one anchor that matches the buyer's stated concern. If their concern is audit prep, use the compliance documentation anchor. If their concern is failed pilots, use the security review anchor.

### The POC pricing conversation (Silverfort parallel)

At Silverfort, Kelan sold POC engagements before platform licenses. This is the same motion:
- Get buy-in on the problem
- Propose a time-boxed engagement to prove the solution in their environment
- Price it so it's low-risk relative to their alternatives
- Use the POC to build internal champions and create expansion evidence

The POC is not charity work. It's a structured deal with defined deliverables and a clear expansion path. If the buyer treats it as free consulting, that's a deal structure problem, not a pricing problem.

### Handling "our budget is locked for this quarter"

This is almost always a proxy for "we're not sure this is worth it yet." The correct response is not to discount. It's to explore:

"Totally understand. What would the decision-making process look like if you did want to move forward? I want to understand the path so we can time this right."

This surfaces whether it's a real budget constraint or a hidden objection. If it's real, ask about Q+1 timing. If it's a hidden objection, you'll hear a different answer.

### How to Say Price (Drill This Out Loud)

**Opening price delivery:**
"The POC engagement is $20,000 over 6 weeks. That gets you one live agent deployed in your environment, a full audit trail, and a ComplianceReport your compliance team can use directly. After POC sign-off, platform starts at $60,000 annually."

**If they ask "is there any flexibility?":**
"I want to understand what's driving the question — is it budget, timeline, or scope? If it's scope, we can look at what we'd adjust. If it's budget, I want to make sure we're solving the right problem for the right number."

Do not offer a discount without understanding why they're asking. Discounts given without understanding the constraint are just margin loss.

**If they ask "what do other customers pay?":**
"It varies based on what they're deploying. The range for mid-market is $60K–$84K annually after POC. The POC is fixed at $20K."

Do not give a lower number than your standard price without a clear reason.

### Value Anchor Matching Guide

| Buyer's Stated Concern | Value Anchor to Use |
|-----------------------|---------------------|
| "We're not sure AI is audit-ready" | ComplianceReport: "We generate in 30 seconds what takes your compliance team 2–3 weeks." |
| "Our security team blocked our last AI project" | SecurityScan: "We produce the artifact your security team needs to approve it. One day, not six months." |
| "We've tried to build this in-house" | In-house build cost: "You're 12 months and $200K–$500K in engineering time away from what we deliver in 6 weeks." |
| "We already have a pilot running" | Pilot purgatory: "How long has it been in pilot? What's blocking production? We solve exactly that problem — the audit trail and compliance layer that gets it across the line." |

**Practice: Pick one anchor and deliver it in 3 sentences.** Not a paragraph. Not a list. Three sentences and then stop.

### Budget Objection Response Scripts

**Objection 1: "We don't have budget this quarter."**

Response:
"Totally understand. Let me ask — is that a hard freeze, or is it more that this hasn't been budgeted because it hasn't been scoped yet? A lot of our customers start with a POC because it fits in a line item that's already open. What does your discretionary spend authority look like?"

Goal: Identify whether this is a real Q freeze or an unbudgeted new thing. If the latter, the POC price ($20K) often fits in an existing discretionary budget.

---

**Objection 2: "This is more than we expected to spend."**

Response:
"I hear you. Let me make sure we're comparing the right things. What were you expecting to spend — and what were you expecting to get? If you were planning to build this internally, the gap closes pretty quickly when you factor in engineering time. If you were planning to use a different platform, I'd like to understand what was in scope there."

Goal: Anchor to the alternative. The comparison isn't "your price vs. zero" — it's "your price vs. what they were going to spend anyway."

---

**Objection 3: "We need to get security and legal involved before we can talk budget."**

Response:
"That's the right process — and we want to support it. The SecurityScan report is designed specifically for that review. Would it help if I sent that over so your security team can look at it before we talk commercial terms? That way they've already approved the platform by the time we're discussing numbers."

Goal: Turn the blocker into a next step. Security review is not a stop — it's a step you can accelerate by pre-staging the SecurityScan artifact.

### Proposal Anatomy

A FlashFusion proposal has six sections. Not more. Not less.

**Section 1 — Problem Statement (half page)**
Echo back what the buyer told you. Their words, not ours. "Your team has been running an AI pilot for 4 months that's blocked from production because of compliance concerns." This signals that you were listening, not just pitching.

**Section 2 — Proposed Solution (half page)**
One agent, one use case, one outcome. "We will deploy a Compliance Copilot agent in your environment over 6 weeks. By the end of the engagement, you will have a live agent, a full audit trail, and a ComplianceReport mapped to SOC 2 CC6 that your team can use in your next audit cycle."

**Section 3 — Deliverables Table**
| Week | Deliverable |
|------|------------|
| 1 | Environment setup + agent configuration |
| 2–3 | Agent testing + knowledge base integration |
| 4–5 | Live deployment + audit trail validation |
| 6 | ComplianceReport generation + security review |

**Section 4 — Investment**
POC: $20,000 (50% at kickoff, 50% at delivery)
Platform Y1: $72,000/year (billed annually, begins at POC sign-off)
Total Year 1: $92,000

No line items. No per-seat pricing. No options matrix. One number, one structure.

**Section 5 — Why Now**
One paragraph on timing. "Your Q3 audit is [date]. To have the ComplianceReport ready for that audit, we need to start no later than [POC kickoff date]." Create a real deadline if one exists.

**Section 6 — Next Steps**
"To move forward, we need: (1) signed SOW, (2) 50% deposit, (3) kickoff call scheduled. Expected timeline from decision to kickoff: 5 business days."

Proposals that end with "let me know if you have questions" get ghosted. Proposals that end with three specific next steps get responded to.

---

### Proposal DOs and DON'Ts

**DO:**
- Use their words in the problem statement
- Include a specific timeline anchor (upcoming audit, quarter end, product launch)
- State one clear number, not a range
- Include next steps with owner and timeline

**DON'T:**
- Include a product features list (that's a spec sheet, not a proposal)
- Offer multiple pricing tiers in the proposal (decide what the right package is before you send)
- Send a proposal to someone who hasn't agreed there's a problem worth solving
- Send a proposal without a follow-up call scheduled

## Common Questions / Resistance

**"Should I send a proposal after every demo?"**
No. Send a proposal when a buyer has acknowledged the problem, seen the solution, and confirmed they have authority to move forward. A proposal sent to someone who can't approve budget is just a free document they'll use to shop you.

**"What if they want a lower price?"**
Ask what's driving that before offering anything. "What would need to be different about this engagement for the number to work?" Sometimes the answer is a reduced scope (one agent instead of two, fewer integrations). Sometimes the answer reveals a hidden objection. Rarely is it actually about price.

**"What if they disappear after the proposal?"**
Follow up twice, 5 business days apart. First follow-up: "Did the proposal land okay? Any questions I can answer?" Second follow-up: "I want to make sure we're respecting the timeline — the audit date is approaching. Is there a next step that makes sense?" If no response to either: "Happy to close this out if the timing isn't right — just let me know and we can revisit when things shift." Then stop.

**"Can we do a free pilot?"**
"We don't do unpaid work, but the POC is structured to be low-risk — you only pay the full amount when we deliver the ComplianceReport. If we don't deliver, you don't pay the second 50%. Does that address the concern?"
