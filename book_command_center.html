Subject: Query: Breathe Through the Bullshit — Literary Memoir My name is Kyle Rosebrook. I have no degree. No credentials. No license. What I have is 187 pages of documented proof that a person can have their mind broken — by injury, by addiction, by circumstance — and build something functional from the wreckage using completely different materials than the original. Breathe Through the Bullshit is a literary memoir about construction. Not recovery. Construction. Here's the distinction the book earns its title on: recovery assumes a baseline worth returning to. After a traumatic brain injury, that baseline doesn't exist anymore. The hardware changed. So the question the book keeps turning over isn't how do I get back to who I was — it's can you build a self from scratch, on purpose, using external scaffolding where internal memory used to be? The answer, it turns out, is yes. But it costs things the productivity books don't mention. The path that produced this answer looks like this: bartending at O'Toole's — reading rooms the way engineers read systems, before I knew what engineers were. Quality control work held together with compensatory strategies I invented out of necessity. Addiction that was simultaneously cause and consequence of cognitive disruption. A TBI that rewrote the operating conditions of my own mind. And then, on the other side of all of it: 240-plus tools configured as externalized cognition. Obsessive documentation as prosthetic memory. Self-taught technical fluency that arrived not despite the injury but partly because of the systems I had to build around it. The person who exists now wasn't recovered. He was constructed. Running parallel to that construction — and illuminating it — is Chaos Club Co., a mental health platform I'm building for people who've stopped pretending chaos is a phase they'll grow out of. The platform's entire philosophy emerged from the same place this book did. The book and the business are the same project. One is the story. One is the infrastructure for people who need the same story told differently. Breathe Through the Bullshit sits alongside Andrew Solomon's The Noonday Demon in its commitment to intellectual rigor about interior experience, Gabor Maté's In the Realm of Hungry Ghosts in its refusal to moralize the wreckage, and Susannah Cahalan's Brain on Fire in its willingness to report from inside cognitive disruption. What distinguishes it is the systems angle. Most memoirs about broken minds follow the medical and emotional arc. This one centers the engineering response — what gets built when the original equipment can't be trusted and nobody's coming to fix it. One more thing worth stating plainly: I can't lie. Not because of character, though I'd like to think that's part of it. Because of the injury. Half the days I can't remember what I had for lunch. Keeping a false narrative coherent across 187 pages, across years, across the testimonies of every person in this story — it's structurally impossible. What's in this manuscript is what happened. That's not a selling point. It's just the only option available to me, and I've made peace with it. The manuscript is complete at approximately [WORD COUNT] words. I'd be glad to send pages, a proposal, or the full manuscript at your request. Kyle Rosebrook kylerosebrook.com [email] [phone]

WHAT MAKES THIS BOOK DIFFERENT
Most memoirs about broken minds follow the medical and emotional arc. This one centers the engineering response — what a person builds when the original equipment can't be trusted.
The systems angle is the unique contribution. Solomon does intellectual rigor. Cahalan does first-person reporting from inside disruption. Maté does addiction without moralism. None of them ask: what do you build, systematically, deliberately, as an engineering project, when the mind you're rebuilding WITH is also the mind that's broken?
