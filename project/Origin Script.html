7–9 MIN · AVATAR #1
PASTE-READY
PASTE INTO HEYGEN

I want to tell you who I am.

Not the version that fits on a résumé. The actual one.

I spent about sixteen years running from something I couldn't name.

It started as marijuana. Then benzos — prescribed, then not. Then opiates. Then cocaine. Then heroin.

That's not a confession. That's just the sequence.

What was underneath all of it was trauma I didn't have the tools to deal with. So I dealt with it the only way I knew how. And that worked — until it didn't. And then it really didn't.

I lost time. I lost relationships. I lost my family's trust. I was homeless. I lived in a Salvation Army. I lived in hotels I couldn't afford the next night on.

And I have a daughter. Kinsley.

She was born in 2018. The day she arrived, I stopped smoking marijuana. Just stopped. Over twenty years. All day. Every day. And then she existed. And I stopped.

That was the first construction. Before I knew what construction meant.

In 2021, I overdosed four times.

The first time I was driving. I did heroin in a dealer's car, and he had to pull me out of the front seat.

The second time, a woman named Mackenzie climbed onto a roof, jimmied a window open, and called an ambulance. She saved my life.

By the last one — I woke up in a hospital in pajama bottoms soaked in my own piss. Both my parents were on either side of me.

Neither of them would look at me.

That was the fall of 2021. For about a year after that, I was trying to figure out how to still be alive.

Then my mom found Amen Clinics. She sent me. They did a SPECT scan. Cognitive testing. The results came back.

Processing speed: ninety-ninth percentile. Recall memory: twelfth percentile.

The engine was intact. The storage was gone.

I didn't find out what those numbers actually meant until four years later. When I read them, I sobbed. Not because of what I'd lost. Because I finally understood what I'd been building against.

I didn't recover from that. You can't recover what you can't verify was there to begin with.

What I did was bartend at O'Toole's for three and a half years. Walk five miles a shift. Read rooms. Read people. Keep the diagnostic instinct running on a brain that couldn't hold what it learned.

And somewhere in there — I found AI. Not because I was looking for a career. Because I needed tools that could remember what I couldn't.

I taught myself. No CS degree. No peers to bounce ideas off. No one to tell me if what I was doing was real. Just a method I worked out alone — screenshot it, copy it, build it, break it, repeat.

The tools weren't productivity hacks. They were external memory for a brain I couldn't trust internally.

After my injury, I decided to learn piano. I'd tried as a kid — nine, ten, eleven years old — and could never get both hands to work together.

I found the most patient teacher I've ever met. A man named Jason.

I learned one song. Nuvole Bianche. White Clouds. By Ludovico Einaudi. Learned it entirely by ear. Took about a year. Never learned to read a single note of music.

It was beautiful.

When I found AI, I stopped playing. I can't remember the song anymore.

I don't know exactly what that means. But I know it's true.

Before O'Toole's ended, I'd already started building. Tools. Systems. A method for working with a brain I couldn't trust.

When I left the bar, I took a QC role — inspecting products, designing defect standards. The same pattern: find what's broken before the system catches it. Different room.

That lasted about six months. And then last fall, I applied for a customer service job at an MSP.

I had no idea support was done by email. I thought it was phone. Didn't matter. I got to work.

Before the first interview, I used agent mode to crawl their entire website. Every page. Affiliates. Clients. Partners. Case studies. I pulled employee names. Then I went to LinkedIn and pulled their partners' employee names too.

I used Gemini to build realistic personas — real people, at real companies, at real levels, facing real problems — the kinds of tickets that would actually come into a support desk from that specific client base.

Then I used Claude to reverse-engineer their tech stack. And started learning it. For a customer service job.

The process was four rounds over a month and a half. All video calls.

At some point I was on a call with the president of the company. And I walked him through everything I'd built. What I'd gathered. How. What tools. Who I'd mapped. I even mentioned the college he went to. Because I'd looked.

Around week four, I started spiraling. Convinced myself I'd imagined being good at something I wasn't actually good at. It got dark.

That's what happens when you've been building alone with no one to reflect it back at you. No peers. No institution. No benchmark. Just you, and the work, and the question of whether any of it is real.

I didn't have an answer. I just kept going.

They didn't hire me for the CSR role. They hired me for a different position — one I hadn't applied for — because of how I thought about the problem.

My boss later put it in my performance review. In writing. Company AI subject matter expert. I was technically still an L1.

I've been reading rooms my whole life. At O'Toole's. Inspecting products. In that interview. Same instinct. Every time. The tools gave it somewhere to go.

I was minutes from dying. Not once. Not twice. Four times.

Kinsley almost grew up without me ever getting to know her. I'm still here. She knows who I am. And I'm just getting started.

Construction over recovery.

DELIVERY — KEY BEATS

  • "Neither of them would look at me." Full stop. No explanation. Let it breathe.
  • The SPECT numbers — slow. 99th, pause. 12th, pause. The gap is the whole story.
  • "I didn't recover. I built something different." Flat. Not defiant. Just true.
  • "I can't remember the song anymore." Say it simply. Don't comfort the audience through it.
  • "Construction over recovery." Quiet ownership. Let it sit. No fanfare.
