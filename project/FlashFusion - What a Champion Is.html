What a Champion Is (and Isn't)

Most AI implementations don't fail because you chose the wrong tools. They fail because you never chose who owns them.

Sep 2026 AI Enablement 5 min read

Every organization I've walked into has AI tools. Almost none of them have anyone who owns the workflow. The tools run. Nobody's responsible for whether they're running well.

You've seen this pattern. You bought the subscriptions, ran an onboarding session, maybe did a lunch-and-learn. Three months later, half the team still does the task the old way. The other half uses the AI, gets inconsistent results, and has no reliable way to improve. Nobody knows who to ask when the output is wrong.

The problem isn't the tool. The problem is that no one chose who owns it. That gap has a name, and it has a fix.

The Ownership Vacuum

When AI adoption stalls, and it does, in most organizations, the diagnosis is usually "change management" or "user resistance." Both are symptoms. The actual cause: there's no one inside the organization whose job it is to know how the AI workflow should run, what to do when it produces unexpected output, and how to help a colleague who's stuck.

That's not a technology problem. You can't fix it with a better tool, a different vendor, or more training sessions. You need a person who takes ownership. That person is a champion.

Three Things That Look Like a Champion But Aren't

Before defining what a champion is, it helps to clear the field.

The AI Enthusiast
The person who follows the newsletters, attended the summit, gets excited about every new model release. Useful for culture and awareness. Not useful for workflow ownership unless they're also embedded in the business domain. Enthusiasm doesn't fix a bad output on a client deliverable.
The IT Lead
IT owns security, governance, and infrastructure, all critical. But IT doesn't carry the domain knowledge of your sales process, your compliance constraints, or why a specific client workflow is built the way it is. Vertical expertise lives somewhere else in the org.
The Power User
The person who uses the AI the most, has figured out some approaches, opens the tool every day. Power users are good at what they know. They're not typically trained to teach, document, or extend what they've learned. And when they leave, the knowledge leaves with them.

What a Champion Actually Is

A champion is a domain expert who has crossed a specific threshold: they understand the work deeply enough to own the AI output, not just use it.

A user asks the AI for something and accepts the result.
An owner asks for something, recognizes whether the result is right, knows how to fix it when it's wrong, and can explain why to a colleague.

Your best champion candidate is almost never the most tech-forward person in the room. It's the person who knows the work so well that bad AI output is immediately obvious to them, and who has the curiosity to figure out what went wrong rather than accepting the result or abandoning the tool.

The Unfair Advantage You Already Have

Your domain experts carry knowledge that no external consultant does: your compliance requirements, your team's culture and risk tolerance, the reasons specific processes were built the way they were, and where the actual friction lives day to day.

When AI knowledge lives inside someone who already carries that domain knowledge, two things happen consistently. Adoption accelerates, because the champion can translate AI capability into terms their team already understands. Quality improves, because the champion recognizes bad output before it causes downstream problems.

The AI ROI formula
AI ROI = adoption rate × correct use rate
An external consultant can move correct use rate temporarily. Only a trained insider can move adoption from the inside, because adoption requires credibility your team has already extended to that person.

The One Characteristic

Technical aptitude helps. Curiosity helps. Organizational credibility helps. But the one thing that separates a champion from a capable power user is simpler: willingness to be wrong out loud, in front of their team.

AI workflows require iteration. You build something, it produces unexpected output, you adjust, and you share what you learned. That cycle requires being visibly imperfect, and being okay with it. Champions who iterate openly build team confidence faster than champions who only share polished results.

If you're scanning your organization right now, look for the person who already says "that didn't work, let me try again" in a meeting. Not the one who only shares wins. That's your candidate.
