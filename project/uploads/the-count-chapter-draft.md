# The Count
There were five of us. I know this the way I know a lot of things now — not because I remember it cleanly, but because when I start pulling the thread, the number holds up. Five. Me, Matt, Tom, Dave, Ryan. The crew. The people I spent a $50,000 insurance settlement on in three or four months, depending on which version of the story my brain serves up on a given Tuesday.
I have a photo from the weekend that killed most of the money. Three guys in suits standing in front of floor-to-ceiling glass, the Chicago skyline doing what the Chicago skyline does when you're spending someone else's money on a high floor of Trump Tower. I'm in the middle — vest, tie, the whole costume. Matt Horne on my left in all black. Tom Ropel on my right. We look like we belong there, which is the entire point of a weekend like that. You don't blow $50K because you need to. You blow it because for 72 hours you get to feel like the kind of person who doesn't have to think about money. Which is a fucked up thing to buy when the money came from getting hit by a car.
Here's how you get $50,000 for a broken wrist: you get high on Xanax, decide to walk to some girl's house because your parents won't let you use the car — because you're in your late twenties living with your parents, because that's where the timeline had deposited you — and a car hits you on the way. You break your wrist. A year or so later, the insurance settlement lands. Fifty thousand dollars. Not life-changing money, but life-disrupting money. Enough to rearrange a few months completely and leave nothing behind but stories you'll tell wrong later.
The car, the drugs, the weekend. Three or four months. Gone.
I paid for everything. I need you to hear that part because it matters later. Not the generosity — I wasn't being generous. I was performing a version of myself that required an audience. The guy who picks up the tab, who books the suite, who says *don't worry about it* because saying that feels like being someone. None of those best friends are in my life anymore.
Except that's not true either. I said it and believed it when I said it, the way you believe something that feels accurate even when the facts don't cooperate. Matt came to Kinsley's fifth birthday party. So did Tom. I reached out to them out of the blue after I moved back, years later, from a completely different life, and they showed up. Matt brought Stephanie — who took the photo that night at Trump Tower — and their two little girls. Tom brought Oliver, his firstborn. They stood in my daughter's yard and ate cake.
But I said *none of them are in my life anymore* first, and I meant it when I said it. The emotional truth arrives before the factual correction. Always. That's not a metaphor for how memory works after a brain injury. That's the literal diagnostic. The feeling-memory and the fact-memory run on different timelines, and the feeling gets there first.
---
Dave Datt wasn't in the photo.
I thought he was. I remembered five people in that frame. I was sure of it — the way I'm sure of a lot of things that turn out to be stitched together from adjacent moments, faces composited over faces, weekends merged into a single weekend. I uploaded the picture and there were three of us. Dave was in India.
Pharmacist school. He went all the way to India to try to become a pharmacist, and he flunked out. Came back to America. And I can't help but line that up next to my own transcript — six years at College of Lake County, ten withdrawals, five failures — because the parallel is loud enough to hear without anyone pointing at it. Two guys from the same crew, both unable to make the institution work, both trying anyway, both flunking out. Dave just did it on a different continent.
He died of a brain aneurysm. Twenties or thirties — I don't have the exact age, and I'm not going to guess because I've already demonstrated tonight what happens when I guess. One day Dave was alive and the next he wasn't, and the thing about an aneurysm is there's no arc to it. No slow decline you can see coming, no intervention window, no version of the story where someone catches it in time. It's a wiring failure. The system just stops.
I went to the funeral. Those years stung when I was standing there. Not because of what Dave and I had — we weren't the closest in the group — but because the years between the Trump Tower weekend and the funeral were the years I was losing everything, and Dave was one of the things I lost without knowing I was losing him. You don't notice someone drifting when you're drowning. You just look up one day and they're not there, and then someone tells you they're never going to be there again, and the grief has a weird shape because you're grieving two things at once — the person and the time you didn't spend with them while they were still a person.
One of my people lost. That's the phrase that came out of me tonight, and it's right. Not *a friend who passed away*. One of my people. Lost. Like a file you know existed but can't locate anymore.
---
Then there was Ryan.
Ryan was the last one I counted. I got to four — me, Matt, Tom, Dave — and sat there with a missing name and nothing to attach it to. Not a face, not a voice, not a bar we went to together. Just the arithmetic of five minus four.
And then the chain kicked in.
This is how my memory works now. Not retrieval — excavation. You don't pull a file. You follow a thread that connects to another thread that catches on something rough enough to hold. The count had a gap. The gap had a shape. The shape was a person I used to hang out with all the time. And when his name surfaced — Ryan — it didn't come alone. It came with his Puerto Rican grandmother's kitchen.
Blue walls. Clean. Cozy in the way that small spaces are cozy when one person has spent decades making them exactly right for one person. Candles and plants. Furniture protectors on most of the cushions. And a single sofa — a sofa-and-a-half, maybe — sitting at an angle in the kitchen-family room, pointed at nothing in particular, oriented the way furniture gets oriented when there's nobody else to negotiate the angle with. You could see the whole story of her life in that sofa's position. She lived alone.
She made empanadas. Rice and beef. I'd clean her gutters because neither Ryan nor she could do it — Ryan was bigger, not built for ladders, and she was somebody's grandmother. So I climbed up there and did the work and she paid me in food. Not because I was being charitable and not because she couldn't pay cash. Because that was the currency that made sense between us. I had a body that could climb. She had a kitchen. The exchange was honest in a way that $50,000 settlements and Trump Tower weekends never were.
I forgot Ryan existed until sixty seconds before I described his grandmother's kitchen in detail I didn't know I still had. Blue paint. Furniture protectors. The angle of the sofa. My brain dropped his name from the roster but kept every object in the room.
---
Ryan's a counselor now. At CLC. At the College of Lake County — the place where I failed and withdrew and failed and withdrew for six years until I finally ground out an associate's degree through sheer stubbornness and a "do the work" kick that also had me building a website on a card table at 1:30 in the morning.
I saw him there during my last semester. Working. He looked focused. Or something else — I couldn't read it. I reached out once after. Nothing back.
And here's where the layers stack up until the structure groans: CLC is where I couldn't hold it together. CLC is where Ryan works. CLC is where I want to go back and teach AI workshops. The guy who got kicked out of NIU for grades, who needed fifteen attempts to finish a two-year degree, wants to walk back into that building as the instructor. And one of his five — the one he forgot — is already there, working as a counselor, in the building, doing a version of the same work.
I'm building a mental health platform. Ryan's practicing mental health in the institution I'm trying to partner with. We haven't spoken in over a decade. The thread is right there and I can't pull it because some threads don't want to be pulled.
---
Five guys. One dead. One unreachable. Two at a birthday party with their kids. And me — the one who made the call, who spent the money, who lost the years, who built the thing after.
I keep thinking about Kinsley's fifth birthday. The same guys who stood in front of that skyline in suits paid for with settlement money, standing in a yard in Mundelein eating grocery store cake. Matt's daughters running around with Kinsley. Tom holding Oliver. Stephanie taking pictures again, the way she always did.
Different skyline. Same people. Minus the ones we lost.
I didn't recover anything that weekend. There was nothing to recover. The guy in the vest at Trump Tower doesn't exist anymore. I didn't bring him back. I called Matt and Tom from a different life — a constructed one, a built one, held together with documentation and tools and custody schedules and a card table in the corner where I teach myself things at 1:30 in the morning — and invited them into it.
They came.
I don't know what that means yet. But I know what it cost to be the one who called.
