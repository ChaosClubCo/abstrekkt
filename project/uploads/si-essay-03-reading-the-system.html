Reading the System

The ticket doesn't tell you what's wrong. It tells you what the user noticed. Those are different problems.

September 2026 Personal essay 5 min read

The ticket doesn't tell you what's wrong. It tells you what the user noticed. Those are different problems.

The bar gave you the person. The floor gave you the object. The ticket queue gave you a description of someone's experience of a problem they couldn't fully name, submitted from a device they may not have understood, about a system they almost certainly didn't see directly.

Three removes from the failure. That's where the work started.

· · ·

The substrate shift between the floor and the queue was the most complete one yet.

On the floor, you still touched the material. You could rotate the shoe. Hold it at a different angle. Look from the other side. Imperfect information, but grounded in something physical that held still while you read it.

In the ticket queue, the substrate was entirely linguistic. Someone had converted a disruption in their workflow into a description — filtered through their vocabulary, their frustration level, their model of how the system was supposed to behave. That description was what you had to work with. You couldn't look at the system from another angle. You could only read what someone said about what they'd experienced.

Language as a substrate is unreliable in ways that shoes aren't. It omits. It emphasizes the wrong things. It carries the observer's assumptions about what's relevant. A user who says "my computer is slow" might be describing a network issue, a memory leak, a failing drive, a misconfigured startup sequence, or the accumulated cost of a difficult morning that had nothing to do with the hardware.

The instinct had to read through the description to the state underneath. Same processing. More noise.

· · ·

The scaffold became load-bearing here in a way it hadn't on the floor.

On the floor, the notebook held defect patterns across shifts. That was the first externalization — memory outside the body, doing the work of accumulation the job required. In the ticket queue, the externalization had to go further. You needed to hold not just what you'd found, but why. Not just the pattern, but the reasoning that connected it to the failure mode. Not just this instance, but the class of problems it represented, so that the next instance — described differently, by a different user, in language you hadn't seen before — would still activate the right diagnostic response.

The cognitive evaluation, years earlier, had said it plainly: pattern recognition intact, recall memory at the 12th percentile. The processor survived what happened to it. The storage didn't. That's a specific operating profile — fast at finding, unreliable at keeping. It runs well with external storage. Without it, the pattern fires but the record isn't there to build on.

The skills library wasn't training material. It was the instinct written down — the diagnostic moves that worked, organized so the next time the situation came up differently dressed, the pattern would be findable without reconstructing the reasoning from scratch. CLAUDE.md wasn't a configuration file. It was working memory: the things that had to be in the system at the start of every session — current context, known failure modes, the shape of the problems in play. The scaffold had moved from holding patterns to holding judgment.

That's a different thing. And it required something the notebook couldn't be.

· · ·

Here's what self-taught means in this context: the instinct found the patterns before the vocabulary arrived to name them.

I didn't learn IT diagnostics and then apply pattern recognition to the domain. The pattern recognition was already running — the same instinct that had read which customer at the bar was about to become a problem, the same instinct that had found defect clusters on the floor before the line confirmed them. When the vocabulary eventually came — through reading, through the terminology that accumulates doing the work — it was mostly naming. Here's what that failure mode is called. Here's the taxonomy the industry uses for what the instinct had already been finding.

A map of territory you've already walked. Useful. But different from the walking.

· · ·

What the queue confirmed: the instinct runs on text.

Not well, at first. Users describe symptoms, not causes. They describe their experience of disruption, filtered through whatever vocabulary and mental model they have. The signal-to-noise ratio is worse than a product on an inspection line. Much worse than a person in a room, whose behavior you could observe directly.

But the instinct adapted. It learned to read vocabulary choices as data — what someone calls a problem tells you something about their model of the system. High frustration in a usually-patient user carries different weight than high frustration in someone who describes everything as broken. It learned the difference between a new problem and a recurring one that had changed its shape enough to look new.

Same instinct. Different substrate. More interference. Still running.

The next domain had no professional requirement attached to it. No job title. No credential that asked for this. Just a piano and the question of whether the instinct cared about any of that.
