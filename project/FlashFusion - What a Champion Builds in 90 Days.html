What a Champion Actually Builds in 90 Days

Ninety days is long enough to know if the model works and short enough that it hasn't cost much to find out. Here's what it actually produced.

Nov 2026 AI Enablement 5 min read

Ninety days is long enough to know if the model works and short enough that it hasn't cost much to find out. That's the honest framing for any AI enablement engagement. By the end of it, a champion has either built something real or the engagement failed, and the difference between those two outcomes is visible before the calendar runs out.

What 90 days actually produces isn't AI fluency. It's not familiarity with a set of tools. It's a small number of specific, owned workflows that the champion runs without help, and the judgment to know when those workflows are producing good output versus bad.

Two verticals. Neither started with structured AI use. The shape of what got built is different in each one. The pattern underneath it is the same.

The first champion runs a residential real estate business and coaches other agents on the side. He came in curious, which meant the question was never whether he'd adopt anything. It was whether what he adopted would hold up well enough that he'd put his name on it in front of people who trust him.

What got built isn't one tool. It's a working set he now runs himself: listing and marketing copy he generates and shapes in his own voice, competitive research that pulls apart what other agents in his market are advertising and adapts the parts worth adapting, and a stack of automations handling the repetitive marketing and search work that used to sit at the bottom of the list because there was never time for it. One piece, a room configurator he opens on an iPad during a showing to show a buyer how their furniture fits the space, is still being built. The rest he uses daily.

The moment that mattered didn't happen in one of our sessions. It happened when he started showing the other agents, walking them through the setup in their own meetings, not as "here's what my consultant built me," but as someone who understood it well enough to teach it. He didn't ask me first. He just did it. That's the engagement closing itself.

The second champion was a different test on purpose. He runs a custom furniture shop, made-to-order seating, built one client at a time. Real estate moves on volume and speed: listings, showings, follow-ups. A custom shop runs on the opposite rhythm. Long lead times, custom quotes, a smaller number of higher-stakes conversations where getting the tone wrong costs a commission, not a click.

His shop runs on NetSuite, so the first thing built was a safe way to connect Claude to it, teaching him to navigate the integration without exposing anything that shouldn't be. From there came custom profiles tuned to how his shop actually talks to clients, the dos and don'ts of writing a prompt that returns something usable the first time, and a reference library he can pull from instead of starting cold. The skills and automations on top of that are still being built out. What's already steady is the writing that used to start from a blank page every time: client updates as a build moves through the shop, and quoting language that explains why a custom piece costs what it costs without apologizing for it.

The vertical is different. The sales cycle, the customer, the stakes of a single conversation, all different. What made it work is identical: one person who knows the work well enough to tell good output from bad, and who keeps iterating instead of accepting the first draft.

The Ownership Signal

The specific moment that closes an engagement isn't a deliverable or a calendar date. It's a behavior change in the champion.

The signal: The champion teaches a colleague without being asked.

Not "here's the workflow my consultant built for me." They explain why it works, what the AI is actually doing, what to do when the output is unexpected, why the workflow is structured the way it is. They're not passing forward a procedure. They're passing forward understanding. That's the moment the knowledge stops being mine and becomes theirs.

Everything before that moment is the engagement. That moment is the exit. When it happens, it's unmistakable, because the champion stops using you as a resource and starts using you as a sounding board. The calls shift from "what do I do here?" to "I figured this out, does this track?" That's the difference between a compliant learner and an owner.

What This Tells You About Your Organization

Two verticals, one pattern. The domain knowledge is different. The regulatory constraints are different. The specific workflows are different. What's the same: one person, deep in the work, with the curiosity to figure out what the AI is actually doing rather than just accepting the output.

1
You need exactly one person
Not a team, not a department, not a budget item. One person who knows the work deeply enough that bad AI output is obvious to them, and who has the disposition to iterate on it rather than abandon it. Most organizations have at least one. The question is whether you've identified them.
2
The technology is not the constraint
In both engagements, the tools were accessible before the engagement started. The constraint was ownership, someone who understood the work deeply enough to use the tool well and build on it over time. The 90 days didn't produce new tools. They produced a person who owns the tools that were already there.
3
The 90-day investment is low relative to the risk
Every organization that deploys AI without an internal owner is running a quiet risk: tools used incorrectly, outputs accepted without evaluation, workflows that work in demos and break in production. A single champion engagement addresses all three. Ninety days is short enough that if the model doesn't work for your context, you'll know before you've spent much to find out.
