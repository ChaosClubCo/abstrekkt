Reading Your Organization's AI Readiness

The gap between "we use AI" and "AI works for us" is almost never a technology problem. It's a diagnostic one. Here's how to read it.

Sep 2026 AI Enablement 5 min read

I ask three questions before I quote any engagement. None of them are about which tools you're using.

Not because your tools don't matter, they do, but because tool questions come after a more important one. The gap between "we use AI" and "AI works for us" is almost never a capability problem. Most organizations have access to the same AI tools. The gap is diagnostic: somebody approved the tools, but nobody owns what good looks like after deployment.

Before I can tell you what an engagement would cost or what it would produce, I need to understand who in your organization owns that problem. Everything else follows from that answer.

What Readiness Actually Means

Most organizations approach AI readiness as a procurement question: Do we have the right tools? Are our licenses current? Is the tech stack compatible? Those are implementation questions. They're second.

The first question is an ownership question. Is there anyone in your organization whose job it is to know whether the AI workflow is running correctly, and what to do when it isn't? Not "who uses it most" and not "who approved it." Who owns the outcome?

Organizations that have a clear answer to that question are ready to do the work. Organizations that don't aren't ready to spend money on tooling yet, because tooling without ownership produces the same result every time: tools used for two weeks, then quietly abandoned.

Three Questions Before I Quote Anything

These three questions come before any scoping conversation. They're not screening questions. They're diagnostic ones, they tell me where the real friction is and whether an engagement would actually produce something.

The three diagnostic questions
Ownership
"Is your AI adoption top-down or bottom-up?"
Top-down means leadership approved a tool and the team is expected to use it. Bottom-up means someone in the work found something that solves a problem they actually own. The second is almost always more real. This tells me who owns the outcome, and whether that person is accessible.
Reality gap
"Where's the gap between what leadership thinks AI is doing and what's actually happening on the ground?"
In almost every organization, the reported use rate is higher than the actual use rate, and the reported impact is higher than the actual impact. The gap between those numbers is where the real friction lives. If someone can name it specifically, that's a good sign. If nobody knows what the gap is, that's the gap.
Accountability
"Who decides what good output looks like? Who catches it when it's wrong?"
This is the question that most often produces silence, and that silence is the finding. When nobody owns the answer, there's no champion. The ownership vacuum shows up here before it shows up anywhere else. Finding it before the engagement starts is more valuable than any tool audit.

What a Champion-Ready Candidate Looks Like

Once the three questions point toward a likely champion candidate, there are three signals that confirm it.

They describe friction, not features.
A champion-ready candidate talks about the work, what takes too long, where errors happen, which handoffs break, not about which AI tools they've heard are good. They have a specific problem they own and a set of constraints around it. Feature conversations are user conversations. Friction conversations are owner conversations.
They mention a constraint before you ask.
Compliance requirements, client sensitivity, internal culture, regulatory limits. If they bring up a constraint unprompted, they're thinking like an owner. Users ask "what can this do?" Owners ask "what are we allowed to do with this, and what happens if we get it wrong?"
They ask what happens when it's wrong.
The most reliable signal. Someone who asks about failure modes before committing to anything is already thinking about ownership, not just utility. That question, "what do we do when the output is bad?", is the ownership instinct surfacing.

The Failure Predictor, and What the Audit Produces

The most reliable predictor of AI implementation failure is a senior leader who demos the tool to the team and calls it adoption. It isn't. That's exposure. The team watches the demo, tries the tool once or twice, and returns to the prior workflow within two weeks, because no one inside the actual workflow owns the outcome.

What we're not measuring: Whether leadership has used the tool.
What we're measuring: Whether anyone at the workflow level has taken ownership of a real problem and has the domain depth to know when the AI is helping versus hurting.

A genuine readiness assessment maps three things: where the highest-friction workflows are and which of them are AI-ready; who the champion candidate is and what's in the way of that person becoming the owner; and what 90 days of structured enablement would produce for that specific person in that specific vertical.

That's the gap map. It's what determines whether an engagement is worth running, and what the engagement should actually produce if it is.
