I woke up drenched in piss. Not the cold, ignorable damp of a spilled drink, but the heavy, sour saturation of total system failure. The hospital bed sheets clung to my legs, adhering the cotton of my pajama bottoms to my skin like wet papier-mâché.

They were sitting on either side of the bed. My parents. Flanking me like stone gargoyles guarding a ruin. Neither of them would look at me directly; their gazes were fixed on middle distances—the humming vital signs monitor, the beige linoleum, anywhere but the epicenter of the collapse. The silence in the room wasn't peaceful; it was the vacuum left behind after an implosion.

The doctors called it a hypoxia event. A dangerous cocktail of Xanax and Ambien that effectively unplugged the mainframes. But that's a clinical euphemism. It wasn't a tragedy; it was a predictable outcome of corrupted hardware. I was overclocking a broken machine, feeding it chemical suppression until the operating system panicked and shut down to protect the core.

The prefrontal cortex—the biological governance module responsible for impulse control—doesn't just pause during active addiction; it degrades. It sustains structural damage.
