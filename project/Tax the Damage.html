A literary memoir in development

Tax the Damage.

The processor survived. The storage didn’t. A literary memoir about addiction, brain injury, fatherhood, and the external systems I built when memory stopped doing its job.

The wrong beginning

The inability to locate it was the beginning.

I tried to tell an AI where this story started.

I said I was nineteen. Then twenty-one. Then nineteen again.

The tool did what tools are trained to do: it cleaned up the contradiction, found a recovery arc, and produced a book called FlashFusion: A Mind Unchained.

Wrong book.

Tax the Damage starts with a memory that cannot keep its own timeline straight, a clinical record that contains its own contradiction, and a man who learned to stop treating uncertainty like a formatting error.

The governing rule is simple: preserve the source before deciding what it means.

The construction

Not recovery. Construction.

My brain did not fail evenly. The processing engine still catches patterns. Recall does not reliably retain them.

I built external memory: documentation, calendars, databases, code, automations, prompts, checkpoints, and systems designed to preserve state when I could not trust myself to hold it. Roughly thirty tools became familiar enough to use as infrastructure. More than 240 configurations were catalogued around them.

Not because I am organized.
Because I cannot afford not to be.

The systems are genuine survival infrastructure. They can also become distance—another layer between a feeling and the person trying to feel it. The book has to hold both facts at once.

The letter

For Kinsley.

This book is a letter to my daughter.

Not an explanation she owes me. Not a request for forgiveness. Not a polished account of how everything worked out.

It is a record of what happened, what I cannot remember, what the documents can support, and what I built afterward. It is evidence of commitment in a form that can still exist when my own memory cannot hold every Friday.

The book does not turn Kinsley into a character. She is the reader, the fixed point, and the reason the construction has to remain connected to something human.
