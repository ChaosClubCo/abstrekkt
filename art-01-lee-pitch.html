The competence story is not the productivity story.

An L1 service desk technician in suburban Illinois caught a multi-tenant isolation failure his employer's enterprise vendor missed. He didn't have a CS degree. He had a method.

The story

In April 2026, while reviewing a vendor-built RLS schema for a customer authentication system, I noticed that one row's tenant_id didn't match the auth context it was returning. I pulled the migration file. The vendor had used a default policy template — same one across every customer's environment. I traced it back. Six months of multi-tenant deployments had been writing to the wrong scope.

The fix took three lines of SQL. The diagnosis took the better part of an afternoon. I'm a self-taught builder working a full-time L1 service desk job. The vendor that built the system had a Series C, an AppSec team, and a name on Gartner's Magic Quadrant.

This is not the kind of story tech press usually tells. Tech press loves the productivity story — "AI helped me write 1,000 lines of code." Or the founder story — "from L1 to CTO in 18 months."

I'm not pitching either of those. I'm pitching the competence story: what happens when the person who actually reads the schema is the person nobody pays to read it.

Why this angle, why now

The vibe-coding narrative dominating tech press right now flattens AI builders into two categories: founders who used Cursor to ship faster, and skeptics who think the whole thing is slop. Both miss what's actually happening at the diagnostic layer.

I've been using Claude as a working partner for fourteen months. Not to write code — I can write code. To pressure-test architecture. To force me to articulate why I think a system is broken before I open a ticket. The output isn't faster shipping. It's better diagnosis.

That distinction matters because it's a different competence story than the one that's been told. And it's one that the readers of Business Insider — operators, technical PMs, mid-career builders — will recognize from their own work, even if no one's named it for them yet.
